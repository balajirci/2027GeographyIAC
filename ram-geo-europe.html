United Kingdom — The Source Package: Study Guide

From Ram's UK Advanced Students Package (Oct 30, 2026) — the documents behind the UK practice sets

Study notes

  • The naming puzzle — Great Britain: geographically, Great Britain is the large island shared by England, Scotland and Wales; politically the name also takes in the smaller surrounding islands such as the Hebrides, Orkney and Shetland. It includes no part of Ireland, and neither the Isle of Man nor the Channel Islands.
  • The naming puzzle — the United Kingdom: the UK's full official name is the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland. It is a sovereign state made up of four countries — England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland — and London is capital both of England and of the UK itself.
  • The naming puzzle — the British Isles: "British Isles" is a purely geographic term for the whole archipelago: it covers Great Britain, the island of Ireland, the Isle of Man, and can also be used to include the Channel Islands. It is not a country or a political unit.
  • The naming puzzle — Ireland: the island of Ireland is shared by Northern Ireland and the Republic of Ireland. After the Irish War of Independence (1919–1921) and the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921, Northern Ireland opted out of the new Irish Free State to stay with Britain, while the south went on to become the fully independent Republic of Ireland.
  • The naming puzzle — Crown Dependencies: the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands (the Bailiwicks of Jersey and Guernsey) are Crown Dependencies: self-governing, and part of neither Great Britain nor the United Kingdom, though the UK looks after their defence and external affairs. The 14 British Overseas Territories (including Gibraltar and the Falkland Islands) are likewise not part of the UK.
  • Rivers — the Severn: the River Severn is the longest river in the UK at 220 miles (354 km). It bubbles out of the ground on the slopes of Plynlimon in Wales and enters the Atlantic Ocean near Bristol in England.
  • Rivers — the Thames and its official source: the Thames runs 215 miles (346 km) from Gloucestershire, through Oxford and London, to the Thames Estuary and the North Sea; it is the deepest river in Britain and navigable as far as London. The Ordnance Survey marks its official source at Thames Head, near the village of Kemble in the Cotswolds, about 5 miles (8 km) south-west of Cirencester.
  • Rivers — the Seven Springs challenge: locals argue the true Thames source is Seven Springs near Cheltenham — the source of the River Churn, which joins the Thames at Cricklade and starts further from the Thames's mouth than Thames Head does. If accepted, the Churn/Thames would gain 14 miles (22 km) and beat the Severn by about nine miles (14 km).
  • Rivers — the Shannon: the longest river in the British Isles is neither the Severn nor the Thames but Ireland's River Shannon, at 360.5 km (224 miles) — a useful reminder that Ireland is not in the United Kingdom.
  • Places & parks — East Anglia: East Anglia is the traditional region of eastern England built on the historic counties of Norfolk and Suffolk (the "north people" and "south people" of the old Anglo-Saxon kingdom), and more loosely includes Cambridgeshire and Essex. Its traditional central town is the cathedral city of Norwich.
  • Places & parks — East Anglia's land and towns: Colchester, in East Anglia, is the oldest recorded town in England and was important in pre-Roman and Roman times. Agriculture still dominates the region: crops have replaced sheep as the mainstay, and barley is the major crop, occupying more land than all other crops combined except in the far south.
  • Places & parks — the Norfolk Broads: the Broads are a network of seven rivers and 63 broads (shallow lakes), most of them navigable, spread over 303 square kilometres and usually less than 4 metres deep; the northern waterways follow the rivers Bure, Ant and Thurne, the southern ones the Waveney, Yare and Chet, and all are tidal.
  • Places & parks — the Limestone Link: the Limestone Link is a 36-mile walking route joining the limestone of the Cotswolds to that of the Mendip Hills, connecting the Cotswold Way at Cold Ashton with the West Mendip Way at Shipham, through the valleys and villages south of Bath.
  • Places & parks — Hadrian's Wall: Hadrian's Wall was essentially the northern border of Roman Britannia — a province that included England and Wales but, for the most part, not Scotland. The wall country runs across the present-day counties of Cumbria and Northumberland, and its wider project area stretched 1,693 square kilometres from Newcastle-upon-Tyne to Bowness-on-Solway, with the Cumbrian coastal defences reaching as far south as Maryport, spanning Cumbria, Northumberland and Tyne and Wear.
  • Places & parks — the Marloes Peninsula: the Marloes Peninsula sits on the west coast of Pembrokeshire at the southern end of St Brides Bay: Dale, on the south side, is tucked into a sheltered bay, while Marloes, on the north side, is wild and exposed. Its cliffs look out to the seabird islands of Skokholm, Skomer and Grassholm.
  • Places & parks — St David's: the St David's Peninsula Heritage Coast runs 50 miles from St Brides Bay to Fishguard. St David's has been a pilgrimage place since the Middle Ages, when two journeys to St David's equalled one journey to Rome; just offshore lies the Ramsey Island nature reserve.
  • Places & parks — the Isles of Scilly: the Isles of Scilly hold over 100 islands, of which only five are inhabited, home to about 2,200 people. Lying some 28 miles from Land's End, they are the southernmost point of the UK.
  • Places & parks — the Channel Tunnel: the Channel Tunnel (the "Chunnel") is a 50.5 km (31.4 mi) rail tunnel linking Folkestone, Kent, with Coquelles near Calais in France, beneath the English Channel at the Strait of Dover. Its 37.9 km (23.5 mi) undersea portion is the longest of any tunnel in the world; there are three parallel bores (two rail tunnels and a smaller service tunnel), the crossing takes about 35 minutes, and the Queen and President Mitterrand officially opened it on May 6, 1994, after construction from 1988 to 1994.

Traps & confusables

  • Great Britain is not the United Kingdom: Great Britain excludes Ireland entirely; the UK is Great Britain plus Northern Ireland. Using the two names interchangeably is the classic error this package is built to catch.
  • "British Isles" is geography, not politics: the term sweeps in the Republic of Ireland, which is a separate sovereign country and no part of the UK — one reason some prefer "Britain and Ireland" or "British-Irish Isles."
  • The Isle of Man and Channel Islands are not in the UK: they are self-governing Crown Dependencies; the UK handles only their defence and external affairs.
  • Severn vs Thames vs Shannon: longest river in the UK = the Severn (220 mi) on textbook figures, with the Thames second (215 mi) — unless the Seven Springs source is accepted, which flips them; longest in the British Isles = the Shannon (224 mi), and it is in Ireland, not the UK.
  • Two rival Thames sources: both Thames Head and Seven Springs carry plaques claiming to mark the source of the Thames — the whole Severn-Thames argument turns on which one counts.
  • Britannia stopped at the Wall: Roman Britannia covered England and Wales but for the most part not Scotland; Hadrian's Wall marked its northern edge.
  • London Bridge is not Tower Bridge: the famous twin-towered bridge in the photographs is Tower Bridge (opened 1894, the only Thames bridge that can be raised); London Bridge has no towers, and the current one opened in 1973.

Self-test

Q1. What is the full official name of the United Kingdom?

Answer

The United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland.

Q2. Which three countries share the island of Great Britain?

Answer

England, Scotland and Wales.

Q3. Is any part of Ireland included in Great Britain?

Answer

No. Great Britain includes no part of Ireland; the UK adds Northern Ireland to Great Britain, while the Republic of Ireland is a separate sovereign country.

Q4. What are the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands, politically?

Answer

Crown Dependencies — self-governing, and part of neither Great Britain nor the United Kingdom; the UK is responsible for their defence and external affairs.

Q5. On textbook figures, what is the longest river in the UK, and how long is it?

Answer

The River Severn, at 220 miles (354 km), rising on the slopes of Plynlimon in Wales.

Q6. Where is the official (Ordnance Survey) source of the River Thames?

Answer

Thames Head, near the village of Kemble in the Cotswolds, about 5 miles (8 km) south-west of Cirencester.

Q7. What claim about Seven Springs would make the Thames longer than the Severn?

Answer

Seven Springs, near Cheltenham, is the source of the River Churn, which flows into the Thames at Cricklade; counting the Churn as the Thames's true start adds 14 miles, putting the Thames about nine miles ahead of the Severn.

Q8. What is the longest river in the British Isles?

Answer

The River Shannon in Ireland, at 360.5 km (224 miles).

Q9. Which two upland areas does the Limestone Link join, between which two long-distance paths?

Answer

It joins the limestone of the Cotswolds to that of the Mendip Hills, linking the Cotswold Way at Cold Ashton to the West Mendip Way at Shipham, over 36 miles.

Q10. What are the Norfolk Broads made up of?

Answer

Seven rivers and 63 broads (shallow lakes), most of them navigable and usually less than 4 metres deep.

Q11. Which two historic counties form the core of East Anglia, and what is its traditional central town?

Answer

Norfolk and Suffolk (Cambridgeshire and Essex are included more loosely); the traditional central town is the cathedral city of Norwich.

Q12. What does the Channel Tunnel connect, and what record does its undersea section hold?

Answer

It links Folkestone, Kent, with Coquelles near Calais, France, under the Strait of Dover; at 37.9 km (23.5 mi), its undersea portion is the longest of any tunnel in the world.

Germany — Study Guide (Readings)

From Ram's Germany advanced extra reading (Sep 2026)

Study notes

  • The Frisian chain — how it formed: the Frisian Islands are a chain of low-lying barrier islands on the edge of the North Sea. Violent storms, flooding and erosion over many centuries washed away or covered land and separated what remained from the mainland by the shallow waters of the Wadden Sea.
  • West Frisian Islands (Netherlands): the Dutch group has five inhabited islands — Texel, Vlieland, Terschelling, Ameland and Schiermonnikoog — with only small villages and scattered farmland. A large swathe of Texel is an important nature preserve reclaimed from the Waddenzee (Wadden Sea) by an extensive series of dikes.
  • West Frisian Islands — getting around: cycling is the main form of transportation on most of the islands, and on Vlieland and Schiermonnikoog only residents are allowed to use their cars. The ferry from Harlingen to Texel takes about 90 minutes.
  • East Frisian Islands (Germany): Germany's group lies east of the Dutch West Frisian Islands. The towns of Borkum and Norderney are the most popular resort areas; the islands are used a little for farming and ranching but mostly as a tourist destination, served by regular ferries and by Norderney airport.
  • North Frisian Islands (Germany): further north again, this German group also embraces Denmark's Fanø, Mandø and Rømø islands within the wider chain. Sylt, the most visited of the North Frisian Islands, is connected to the mainland by a long drivable embankment and also has an airport.
  • North Frisian Islands — the lost island of Strand: from Langeness south to Pellworm Island and the Nordstrand Peninsula, the islands are the remains of the much larger island of Strand, torn apart by great storm tidal surges; the surviving fragments are called the Halligen Islands, while hundreds of pieces of Strand remain submerged.
  • Bavaria — the basics: Bavaria, in the southeast of Germany, is the largest of Germany's 16 federal states: almost one-fifth of the country's total land area at 27,200 square miles, with 12.5 million inhabitants. Its capital is Munich (München); other major cities include Nuremberg (Nürnberg), Augsburg, Würzburg, Regensburg, Ingolstadt, Fürth and Erlangen.
  • Bavaria — borders: Bavaria borders Austria, the Czech Republic and Switzerland (across Lake Constance), as well as the German states of Baden-Württemberg, Hesse, Thuringia and Saxony.
  • Bavaria — rivers and highest peak: the two major rivers flowing through Bavaria are the Danube (Donau) and the Main. The Bavarian Alps, forming a natural border with Austria, hold Germany's highest peak, the Zugspitze at 9,717 feet.
  • Bavaria — four landscape regions: the Alps with the Zugspitze; the Alpine foothills with their numerous lakes; the Eastern Bavarian central mountains, home to the first ever national park in Germany; and the Swabian-Franconian cuesta landscape.
  • Bavaria — measurement and nature: Bavaria was the first region in Europe ever to be precisely measured, and nature conservation and environmental protection have been an integral part of the Bavarian constitution since 1984.
  • Passau — City of Three Rivers: Passau sits in the southern foothills of the Bavarian Forest at the confluence of three rivers — the Danube, the Inn and the Ilz. Razed in the great fire of 1662 and rebuilt to designs by Italian architects, it has a Mediterranean baroque flair and is nicknamed the "Venice of Bavaria"; its St Stephen's Cathedral is the largest baroque cathedral north of the Alps and holds the world's largest cathedral pipe organ.
  • Usedom — the divided island: Usedom lies in the far northeast of Germany between the Baltic Sea and the Stettin Lagoon (Stettiner Haff), in the delta of the Oder River about 30 km east of Greifswald. It is Germany's second biggest island after Rügen and, as the sunniest region of Germany, is called the "sunshine island." Since 1945 the island has been split: about 80% belongs to Germany (373 of its 445 sq km), while the eastern tip, including the town of Świnoujście (Swinemünde), is Polish — and the Polish side, with about 45,000 residents, actually holds more people than the German side's roughly 30,000.

Self-test

Q1. Which country do the West Frisian Islands belong to, and which five of them are inhabited?

Answer

The Netherlands; the inhabited islands are Texel, Vlieland, Terschelling, Ameland and Schiermonnikoog.

Q2. On which two West Frisian islands may only residents use cars?

Answer

Vlieland and Schiermonnikoog; cycling is the main transport across the group.

Q3. Which is the most visited of the North Frisian Islands, and how is it joined to the mainland?

Answer

Sylt, connected to the mainland by a long drivable embankment (it also has an airport and ferry services).

Q4. The Halligen Islands are the surviving fragments of which lost island, and what destroyed it?

Answer

The much larger island of Strand, torn apart by great storm tidal surges and flooding.

Q5. What is Germany's largest federal state, and what is its capital?

Answer

Bavaria — almost one-fifth of Germany's land area in the southeast; its capital is Munich (München).

Q6. What is Germany's highest peak, how high is it, and where does it stand?

Answer

The Zugspitze, 9,717 feet, in the Bavarian Alps on the natural border with Austria.

Q7. Which three rivers meet at Passau, the "City of Three Rivers"?

Answer

The Danube, the Inn and the Ilz.

Q8. Usedom is Germany's second largest island after which island, and with which country is it shared?

Answer

After Rügen; Usedom is shared with Poland, which has held the eastern tip, including Świnoujście, since 1945 (about 80% of the island is German).
