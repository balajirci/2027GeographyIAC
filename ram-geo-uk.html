Part A — Answers (tap to reveal)

1. B — England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland together make up the UK.

2. C — The Republic of Ireland is an independent country; the red border line on the maps separates it from Northern Ireland.

3. B — 4,406 ft / 1,343 m. (Snowdon is 1,086 m, Carrauntoohill 1,041 m, and the Brecon Beacons rise to about 610 m.)

4. B — Cheddar Gorge is in the Mendip Hills, on the northern edge of the Somerset Plain.

5. B — It joins the limestone of the Cotswolds to the limestone of the Mendip Hills.

6. A — East Anglia is the traditional region of Norfolk and Suffolk, and, more loosely, Cambridgeshire and Essex.

7. B — Seven rivers and 63 broads (lakes), usually less than 4 m deep.

8. B — Crops have replaced sheep as the mainstay of agriculture, and barley is the major crop.

9. C — Coquelles is in Pas-de-Calais, near Calais.

10. B — Its undersea portion is 37.9 km (23.5 mi). The longest tunnel overall is the Delaware Aqueduct.

11. B — Five inhabited islands (home to about 2,200 people) and countless uninhabited ones.

12. C — Lerwick is on Mainland Shetland. Kirkwall is in Orkney and Stornoway is on the Isle of Lewis.

13. B — Antrim, Armagh, Down, Fermanagh, Londonderry (Derry) and Tyrone.

14. A — Lough Neagh is the largest lake in the British Isles ✦.

15. C — Edinburgh is Scotland's capital, Cardiff is Wales's, London is England's and Dublin is the Republic of Ireland's.

17. B — The Prime Meridian (0° longitude) passes through Greenwich, in London.

18. B — It is labelled on the Europe 1805 map beside Cape Trafalgar and Gibraltar.

19. B — A small opening in the roof lets sunlight illuminate the interior at winter solstice. The Book of Kells is at Trinity College Dublin.

S1. Great Britain is the main island (England, Scotland, Wales). The UK is those three plus Northern Ireland. The British Isles is a geographic term for the whole group of islands, including the island of Ireland and many smaller islands. S2. Any three of: Atlantic Ocean, North Sea, Irish Sea, English Channel, Bristol Channel, St. George's Channel. S3. They are built from local golden oolite limestone (Burford and Oxford are examples). S12. Dale is tucked away in a sheltered bay; Marloes is in a much wilder, more exposed location.

Part D — Answers (tap to reveal)

L1. Scotland: North West Highlands, Grampian Mountains, Cheviot Hills on the border, Ben Nevis 1,343 m. Wales: Cambrian Mountains, Mt. Snowdon 1,086 m, Brecon Beacons and Black Mountains in the south. England: Pennines, Lake District/Cumbrian Mountains, Yorkshire Moors, Dartmoor and Mendip Hills in the southwest. Lowlands: south-east and east England (the Thames valley, The Wash, East Anglia). L2. UK: four countries — England, Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland — with capitals London, Edinburgh, Cardiff, Belfast. Republic of Ireland: an independent country with its capital at Dublin, separated from Northern Ireland by a land border. Isle of Man and the Channel Islands: British Crown Dependencies (Douglas, St. Helier, St. Peter Port). L3. Benefits: fast crossing (about 35 minutes), up to 400 trains per day, record 21 million passengers in 2014, freight, pet travel scheme, engineering achievement. Problems: cost 80% over budget (£4.65 billion), fires in 1996, 2006 and 2012, the December 2009 breakdowns trapping 2,000 passengers, ten workers killed during construction. L4. Features: seven rivers, 63 broads, shallow, tidal, nature reserves. Uses: boating and sailing, tourism (yacht stations, museums), wildlife. Concern: pressure from boating, tidal flooding, and farming — barley is the major crop of the region. Accept any reasoned concern. L5. Examples: Newgrange (prehistoric tomb, c. 3000 B.C., solstice light); Skellig Michael (early Christian monastery); Trinity College and the Book of Kells (learning and manuscripts); Galway Festival and Lisdoonvarna (living traditions of music and dance); Aran Islands (Gaelic language). L6. National Parks are mostly in upland or scenic areas (Scotland's Highlands, Lake District, Snowdonia, Peak District) and some coasts; AONBs are many smaller areas, especially along coasts and in southern England and Northern Ireland. Reasons: rugged uplands, scenic coasts, rural heritage, low population in uplands.
