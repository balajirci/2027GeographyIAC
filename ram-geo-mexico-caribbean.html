Ram's Geo Extra — Mexico & the Caribbean

Study guides distilled from the Mexico supplements and the Antilles notes

Mexico — Study Guide

From Ram's Mexico supplements (Jan 2026)

Study notes

The Aztec capital — Tenochtitlan

  • Who the Aztecs were: The Aztecs called themselves the Mexica. They were not originally from the Valley of Mexico — they migrated from the north, from a homeland they called Aztlan, "The Place of Herons," which has never been identified archaeologically and was likely at least partly mythical. On the migration they carried the idol of their patron deity Huitzilopochtli; after about two centuries of wandering they reached the Valley of Mexico around AD 1250.
  • The valley setting: The Valley (Basin) of Mexico lies about 2,100 metres (7,000 feet) above sea level, ringed by high mountains, some of them active volcanoes. Streams running down from the mountains formed a series of shallow, marshy lakes rich in fish, animals, plants and salt.
  • Expelled from Chapultepec: The late-coming Mexica were forced to settle on the inhospitable hill of Chapultepec (Grasshopper Hill) as vassals of Culhuacan. After the sacrifice and flaying of the Culhua king's daughter — demanded, the Mexica said, by Huitzilopochtli — they lost the ensuing battle and were driven out to marshy islands in the middle of the lake.
  • Founding of Tenochtitlan: Following Huitzilopochtli's sign — a great eagle perched on a cactus killing a snake — the Mexica founded their capital, Tenochtitlan, in AD 1325 (the year 2 Calli, "Two House," in the Aztec calendar), on marshland in the lake. The awkward site protected the city, since access was only by canoe or boat, and it grew rapidly into a commercial and military centre.
  • The Templo Mayor: At the city's ceremonial heart stood the Great Temple (Templo Mayor, or Huey Teocalli, "Great House of the Gods") — a stepped pyramid with a double temple on top dedicated to Huitzilopochtli and Tlaloc. It was rebuilt many times; the seventh and final version was the one Cortés saw when he entered the capital on November 8, 1519, and found one of the largest cities in the world. Aqueducts brought fresh water in from the mountains, and today's Mexico City sits on top of Tenochtitlan's ruins.

Teotihuacan

  • What and where: Teotihuacan is a ruined holy city about 50 km (30 miles) northeast of Mexico City, made a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987. Its name is Nahuatl for "birthplace of the gods" — a name the Aztecs gave it centuries after its fall, because they believed the gods created the universe there. Its real name is unknown; Maya hieroglyphic texts call it puh, "Place of Reeds."
  • Age and size: Settled as early as about 400–200 BC, Teotihuacan flourished from the 1st to the 7th centuries AD, reaching its zenith roughly between 100 BC and AD 650. It covered about 8 square miles (21 sq km) and held perhaps 100,000 to 250,000 people — the largest city anywhere in the Western Hemisphere before the 1400s.
  • The great monuments: The Pyramid of the Sun (completed by about 100 CE) is the city's largest structure and the third-largest pyramid in the world; the Pyramid of the Moon stands at the north end of the site; and the Temple of Quetzalcoatl (the Feathered Serpent) sits in the plaza the Spanish named the Citadel. Its stepped-pyramid style was later adopted by the Maya and the Aztecs.
  • The Avenue of the Dead: The city's broad central avenue is flanked by smaller talud-tablero (stepped) platforms. The Aztecs believed these platforms were tombs, which is why they called it the Avenue of the Dead; they were actually ceremonial platforms topped with temples. The whole urban grid is aligned precisely 15.5 degrees east of north, and the avenue lines up with Cerro Gordo mountain.
  • Who built it — and how it ended: The builders are still debated. Archaeologists once credited the Toltecs, but Teotihuacan predates the Toltec civilization, ruling them out; the Totonac have also been proposed, and the city in fact hosted a patchwork of peoples — Maya, Mixtec, Zapotec and others. One theory says refugees from Cuicuilco, fleeing volcanic activity, founded or bolstered it. Remarkably, the city had no fortifications. Sometime in the 7th or 8th century CE it was sacked and burned, by invaders or in an internal uprising.
  • Why the Aztecs revered it: The Aztecs treated the abandoned city as the sacred setting for the creation of the current world, the Fifth Sun. They carried off and reused its objects — more than 40 Teotihuacan-style objects have been found in offerings inside Tenochtitlan's ceremonial precinct.

The Toltec–Maya link

  • The Toltecs and Tula: After Teotihuacan fell and before the Aztecs rose, the Toltecs were one of the great powers of the Basin of Mexico. Their capital was Tula (in modern Hidalgo state), the early post-classic Toltec capital from about AD 950 to 1150; in their heyday (about AD 900–1200) they controlled most of central Mexico, the Yucatán peninsula and the Gulf coast, with trade reaching as far as modern New Mexico, Arizona, Costa Rica and Guatemala. The Aztecs revered the Toltecs as ideal rulers — Motecuhzoma even sent men to dig up Toltec objects for Tenochtitlan's temples — and called both Tula and Teotihuacan "the Tollans." In Nahuatl, "Toltec" also simply means "great craftsman."
  • Toltec style arrives at Chichén Itzá: Beginning about 950 AD, a new style of architecture — and with it people and culture — appeared at the Maya city of Chichén Itzá in Yucatán. The result of this influx of Toltec people was probably a ruling class mixing Yucatec Maya, Toltecs and Itzas.
  • Toltec features to recognise: The feathered (plumed) serpent, called Kukulcan in Maya and Quetzalcoatl in Nahuatl; chacmools; the Tzompantli skull rack; and images of Toltec warriors. Architecturally: colonnades and columned halls with wall benches. The Toltecs are also probably behind Chichén Itzá's increased emphasis on warfare, human sacrifice and death culture.
  • The talud-tablero thread: Chichén Itzá's pyramids, such as El Castillo, are built of stacked platforms of decreasing size in the talud-tablero style — the angled, stair-step platform profile that had originally developed at Teotihuacan, linking all three cultures in one building technique.

Traps & confusables

  • Teotihuacan is NOT Tenochtitlan: Teotihuacan is the far older city (flourishing centuries before the Aztecs existed as a power), already abandoned for hundreds of years when the Mexica arrived. The Aztecs did not build it or rule from it — they only named it, worshipped at it and mined it for sacred objects. Tenochtitlan is the actual Aztec capital, founded in 1325 on lake islands where Mexico City now stands; Teotihuacan is a ruin some 50 km northeast of the modern city.
  • The Toltecs did not build Teotihuacan: Aztec writings credited the Toltecs, but the Toltec peak came far later than Teotihuacan's zenith. The Toltec capital was Tula, a separate, later city.
  • Chichén Itzá is a Maya city with Toltec features: The feathered serpent, chacmools, skull rack and colonnades arrived with Toltec influence from about 950 AD — they do not make it a Toltec capital. Kukulcan (Maya) and Quetzalcoatl (Nahuatl) are the same feathered-serpent deity under two names.
  • Avenue of the Dead: The platforms along it are not tombs — that was an Aztec misunderstanding. Likewise the "Citadel" was not a fort; the Spanish misnamed a religious-political plaza.

Self-test

Q1. What does the name Teotihuacan mean, and who gave the city that name?

Answer

It is Nahuatl for "birthplace of the gods." The Aztecs named it centuries after its fall, believing the gods had created the universe there; the city's original name is unknown.

Q2. In what year was Tenochtitlan founded, and what sign told the Mexica where to found it?

Answer

AD 1325 (the year 2 Calli). Huitzilopochtli indicated the spot where a great eagle was perched on a cactus killing a snake — on marshy islands in the middle of the lake.

Q3. The Templo Mayor had a double temple on top. Which two deities was it dedicated to?

Answer

Huitzilopochtli, the Mexica patron deity, and Tlaloc, the rain god.

Q4. What was the Mexica's homeland called, and what does its name mean?

Answer

Aztlan, "The Place of Herons" — a northern homeland that has never been identified archaeologically and was likely at least partly mythical.

Q5. Why were the Mexica forced to leave Chapultepec?

Answer

While vassals of Culhuacan, they sacrificed and flayed the Culhua king's daughter, saying Huitzilopochtli demanded it. The enraged Culhua attacked, defeated the Mexica, and drove them onto the lake's marshy islands.

Q6. Why is Teotihuacan's main street called the Avenue of the Dead?

Answer

The Aztecs believed the stepped talud-tablero platforms lining it were tombs. They were actually ceremonial platforms that had been topped with temples.

Q7. What was the Toltec capital, and roughly when did it flourish?

Answer

Tula (in modern Hidalgo state), the Toltec capital from about AD 950 to 1150, with the Toltec heyday around AD 900–1200.

Q8. Name three Toltec features that appear at Chichén Itzá.

Answer

Any three of: the feathered serpent (Kukulcan/Quetzalcoatl), chacmools, the Tzompantli skull rack, Toltec warrior imagery, and colonnades or columned halls with wall benches.

Q9. What is the talud-tablero style, where did it develop, and where can it be seen at Chichén Itzá?

Answer

It is the angled, stair-step profile of stacked pyramid platforms of decreasing size. It developed at Teotihuacan and can be seen in El Castillo at Chichén Itzá.

Q10. Why do archaeologists now rule out the Toltecs as the builders of Teotihuacan?

Answer

Because Teotihuacan predates the Toltec civilization — it flourished centuries before the Toltec peak, so the Toltecs cannot have founded it, despite what Aztec writings claimed.

The Caribbean & the Antilles — Study Guide

From Ram's Antilles notes (2023-24)

Study notes

  • What the Antilles are: The Antilles are the islands of the Caribbean Sea comprising all of the West Indies except the Bahamas. They divide into two major groups: the Greater Antilles and the Lesser Antilles.
  • The Greater Antilles: Cuba, Hispaniola (shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic), Jamaica, Puerto Rico and — geologically — the Virgin Islands. The four largest islands (Cuba, Hispaniola, Jamaica and Puerto Rico) lie north of the Lesser Antilles chain and make up nearly 90 percent of the total land area of the entire West Indies.
  • The Virgin Islands puzzle: Geologically the Virgin Islands belong to the Greater Antilles — they are part of a submerged mountain chain like the other Greater Antilles — but politically they are counted with the Lesser Antilles, and most atlases place them there. For the geography bee, treat the Virgin Islands as a link between the two groups, part of neither.
  • The Lesser Antilles: The long arc of small islands running north–south in the Caribbean from the Virgin Islands down to Grenada. Definitions usually also sweep in Trinidad and Tobago and the east–west island chain from Margarita Island to Aruba off Venezuela, even though those islands sit physiographically on the South American continental shelf.
  • Northernmost of the Lesser Antilles: Anguilla is regarded as the most northerly of the Lesser Antilles (and the most northerly of the Leeward Islands). Anegada Island in the British Virgin Islands actually lies farther north than Anguilla, but because the Virgin Islands are not counted in the Lesser Antilles, Anegada is not the answer.
  • Why "Windward" and "Leeward": Since early Spanish colonial times the north–south Lesser Antilles have been split into Windward and Leeward groups — a division the French, Dutch and British also adopted. The names reflect the prevailing easterly trade winds: the Windwards face the wind, the Leewards are sheltered from it.
  • The Leeward Islands: The most westerly and northerly of the Lesser Antilles, between about 16° and 19° N, running generally from Guadeloupe to the Virgin Islands. From north to south: Anguilla; Saint Martin (shared between France and the Netherlands); Saint-Barthélemy; Saba; Saint Eustatius; Saint Kitts and Nevis; Antigua and Barbuda; Montserrat; and Guadeloupe with its dependency Marie-Galante. (Many atlases include the US and British Virgin Islands, but geologically they belong to the Greater Antilles.)
  • Leeward geology: Antigua, Anguilla, Barbuda and eastern Guadeloupe are coral limestone, while the small chain from Saint Kitts to Montserrat forms a volcanic ridge. Soufrière on Guadeloupe, at 4,813 feet (1,467 m), is the highest mountain in the Lesser Antilles. The Leeward climate is drier than farther south; hurricanes threaten from June to October.
  • The Windward Islands: The southern arc of the Lesser Antilles, between about 12° and 16° N, stretching from Dominica to Grenada. From north to south: Dominica; Martinique (a French department); Saint Lucia; Saint Vincent; and Grenada — with the small Grenadine islands strung between Saint Vincent and Grenada. They are the tips of a volcanic mountain range raised where the Atlantic and Caribbean plates collided.
  • Dominica: Dominica is the youngest island in the Lesser Antilles and belongs to the Windward Islands — even though the British formerly administered it as part of the Leeward Islands.
  • Barbados stands apart: Barbados lies east of the Windward chain and is the easternmost island in the Caribbean and the West Indies. It is a limestone massif, not volcanic, so it is not part of the volcanic Windward arc (though it is often loosely grouped with the Windwards).
  • Trinidad and Tobago: Geologically an extension of the South American mainland, sitting at the southern end of the chain — but usually NOT counted as part of the Windward Islands. Whether Trinidad belongs to the Lesser Antilles at all is a point even National Geographic has left ambiguous, so listen carefully to the clues in any question.
  • Volcanic violence: The Windwards are volcanic, with craters, hot springs and sulfuric vents. On May 8, 1902, the eruption of Mount Pelée on Martinique killed about 30,000 people — one of the most destructive eruptions in modern history.
  • The Dutch Caribbean: Six islands in two groups. The ABC islands — Aruba, Bonaire and Curaçao, off the Venezuelan coast — are the "Leeward Antilles," an arid east–west chain; the SSS islands — Saba, Sint Eustatius and Sint Maarten — are the Dutch "Windward" islands. The Netherlands Antilles was dissolved on October 10, 2010: Curaçao and Sint Maarten became autonomous countries within the Kingdom of the Netherlands (as Aruba had been since 1986), while Bonaire, Saba and Sint Eustatius became special municipalities of the Netherlands itself.

Self-test

Q1. Which islands make up the Greater Antilles?

Answer

Cuba, Hispaniola (Haiti and the Dominican Republic), Jamaica and Puerto Rico — plus, geologically, the Virgin Islands.

Q2. Which island is regarded as the northernmost of the Lesser Antilles?

Answer

Anguilla. Anegada in the British Virgin Islands lies farther north, but the Virgin Islands are not counted as part of the Lesser Antilles.

Q3. From which island to which island do the Windward Islands stretch?

Answer

From Dominica in the north to Grenada in the south, taking in Martinique, Saint Lucia, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines.

Q4. What is the highest mountain in the Lesser Antilles, and on which island is it?

Answer

Soufrière, on Guadeloupe — 4,813 feet (1,467 m).

Q5. Why is Barbados not considered part of the Windward Islands chain?

Answer

It lies east of the chain and is a limestone massif rather than a volcanic island, so it is not part of the volcanic Windward arc — though it is the easternmost island in the Caribbean.

Q6. Which is the youngest island in the Lesser Antilles, and to which group does it belong?

Answer

Dominica — it belongs to the Windward Islands (despite once being administered by Britain with the Leewards).

Q7. Which three islands are the ABC islands, and by what other name is their group known?

Answer

Aruba, Bonaire and Curaçao — the Leeward Antilles, the arid east–west chain off the coast of Venezuela.

Q8. Which volcano erupted in 1902 killing about 30,000 people, and on which island?

Answer

Mount Pelée, on Martinique, on May 8, 1902 — one of the most destructive eruptions in modern history.
