Ram's Geo Extra — South America Study Guides

Organized study guides built from the October 2026 readings: notes first, then self-test with tap-to-reveal answers

Colombia — Study Guide

From Ram's supplement readings (Oct 2026)

Study notes

  • San Andrés archipelago — what it is: The Archipelago of San Andrés, Providencia and Santa Catalina is Colombia's only oceanic and West Indies department. It consists of two island groups plus eight outlying banks and reefs, with a total land area of just 57 square kilometres and a population of more than 83,000.
  • San Andrés archipelago — location: The islands lie about 775 km (482 mi) northwest of mainland Colombia and only about 220 km (140 mi) from the coast of Nicaragua — Colombian territory sitting far closer to Nicaragua than to Colombia itself.
  • San Andrés island: The largest island of the archipelago is San Andrés, and the departmental capital, also called San Andrés, is on it. It is the densely populated, tourist-centred island of the group.
  • Providencia: This island lies 72 km north of San Andrés and is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve. With only about 4,000 inhabitants, primitive infrastructure and geographical isolation, it is far quieter than San Andrés. A 20-kilometre coral barrier reef borders its coasts, giving splendid diving conditions.
  • Santa Catalina: Providencia's small mountainous sister island, just one square kilometre, connected to Providencia by a 100-metre footbridge known as the Lovers' Bridge. It is the younger sister island to the south end of Providencia.
  • San Andrés archipelago — history: The islands were first settled by English Puritans (arriving around 1627–1629, from Barbados and then England), who settled Old Providence between 1630 and 1641. Slaves first arrived in 1633, and Old Providence became a centre of the slave trade, piracy and contraband — English pirates used it as a refuge to attack Spanish ships until Spain expelled nearly the whole colonial population.
  • Chocó-Darién — the region: This belt of rainforest, mountains and coast runs along the entire Colombian Pacific coast, from southwest Panama to northwest Ecuador. The Panamanian Darién part holds some of the richest forests of Central America, while the Colombian Chocó has some of the highest numbers of endemic plants in South America.
  • Chocó-Darién — wildlife: Unique species of the region include jaguars, ocelots, giant anteaters, tapirs and tamarins.
  • Chocó department — basics: Chocó (inhabitant: Chocoano) is in northwest Colombia, bordering the Pacific Ocean, Panama, the Caribbean Sea, and the departments of Antioquia, Risaralda and Valle del Cauca. It covers 46,530 square kilometres with nearly 650,000 inhabitants, 67 per cent of them rural; most of the population is of African descent.
  • Chocó — four sub-regions: The seaboard strip, the mountainous region of Baudó, the basins of the Atrato and San Juan rivers, and the western side of the Western Cordillera.
  • Chocó — climate and the Darién blockage: Chocó has the region's highest humidity, hot temperatures and intense rain. The thick, flooded rainforest in the Atrato river valley is the Tapón del Darién — the "Darién blockage" (the famous Darién Gap).
  • Chocó — economy: Gold mining is the main income source — Chocó is Colombia's second-largest gold producer — and the San Juan River valley is one of the richest platinum regions in the world. Silver, copper, petroleum and wood are also exploited, with banana, coconut, cacao, rice and corn grown around the Gulf of Urabá.
  • Chocó — places: The capital is Quibdó, founded in 1648 as Citará and made departmental capital in 1828. Natural parks include Utría Inlet, Katíos and Tamaná. Chocó has two seaports: Bahía Solano on the Pacific and Acandí on the Caribbean coast.
  • Chocó — first Spanish contact: Rodrigo de Bastidas was the first Spanish conqueror to reach the region, in 1501. In 1510 Martín Fernández de Enciso founded Santa María la Antigua del Darién, the first Spanish village in Colombian territory.
  • The three "Pico" peaks: The highest mountain in Colombia is Pico Cristóbal Colón, in the Santa Marta Range; the nearby Pico Simón Bolívar, in the same range, is at essentially the same elevation. Pico Bolívar is a different mountain entirely — it is the highest peak of Venezuela, in the Mérida Range.
  • Peak namesakes: Pico Cristóbal Colón is named after Christopher Columbus; Pico Simón Bolívar is named after the revolutionary hero Simón Bolívar.
  • Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta: This range in northern Colombia (departments of Magdalena, La Guajira and Cesar) is the tallest seaside mountain system in the world and contains Colombia's two largest mountains. UNESCO declared the park a Biosphere Reserve and a World Heritage Site in 1979.
  • Sierra Nevada — people and the Lost City: Around 30,000 members of the Kogi, Arhuaco, Kankuamo and Wiwa indigenous communities — known as the "older brothers" — live in the park. It also holds the Teyuna Archaeological Park, the "Ciudad Perdida" (Lost City), cradle of the country's most monumental Indian civilization.
  • Lake Tota: Colombia's largest and most important lake, east of the department of Boyacá, only 15 km south of the city of Sogamoso. Despite an average temperature of about 12 °C, it has white sand beaches that recall the Caribbean.

Traps & confusables

  • Pico Cristóbal Colón vs Pico Simón Bolívar vs Pico Bolívar: Colón = highest in Colombia (Santa Marta Range); Simón Bolívar = near-equal peak in the same Colombian range; plain Pico Bolívar = highest in Venezuela (Mérida Range). If a question says "Pico Bolívar," answer Venezuela.
  • Ram's own instruction: Sources contradict each other on the two Santa Marta peaks (one gives Cristóbal Colón 18,950 ft and Simón Bolívar 18,497 ft). In a bee, give Pico Cristóbal Colón as your first answer for Colombia's highest peak.
  • San Andrés belongs to Colombia, not Nicaragua: The archipelago is much closer to Nicaragua (220 km) than to Colombia (775 km), which is exactly why it gets asked.
  • Providencia vs San Andrés: Providencia is the small, quiet, UNESCO Biosphere Reserve island 72 km north; San Andrés is the large, densely populated tourist island and capital. Santa Catalina (1 km²) hangs off Providencia by the Lovers' Bridge, not off San Andrés.
  • Chocó's two coasts: Chocó is a Pacific department, but it also has a Caribbean shoreline — its port of Acandí is on the Caribbean while Bahía Solano is on the Pacific. It is the only Colombian department context in these readings with ports on both seas.
  • Gold vs platinum: Chocó's headline metal is gold (Colombia's #2 producer); the San Juan River valley's claim to fame is platinum, among the richest platinum regions in the world.

Self-test

Q1. What is Colombia's only oceanic department, made up of Caribbean island groups and outlying banks and reefs?

Answer

The Archipelago of San Andrés, Providencia and Santa Catalina — Colombia's only oceanic and West Indies department.

Q2. The San Andrés archipelago lies about 220 km from the coast of which country — far closer than to mainland Colombia?

Answer

Nicaragua. The islands are about 775 km northwest of mainland Colombia but only about 220 km from Nicaragua.

Q3. Which island of the San Andrés group, 72 km north of San Andrés island, is a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve with a 20-km coral barrier reef?

Answer

Providencia — population only about 4,000, known for diving on its coral barrier.

Q4. Santa Catalina Island is connected to Providencia by a 100-metre footbridge. What is the bridge called?

Answer

The Lovers' Bridge.

Q5. Who were the first settlers of Old Providence and Santa Catalina in 1630–1641?

Answer

English Puritans, who came via Barbados and directly from England.

Q6. The Chocó-Darién region runs along Colombia's Pacific coast between which two countries?

Answer

From southwest Panama to northwest Ecuador.

Q7. What is the Tapón del Darién?

Answer

The thick, flooded rainforest in the valley of the Atrato River in Chocó — the "Darién blockage" (Darién Gap).

Q8. Chocó is Colombia's second-largest producer of which metal, and the San Juan River valley is one of the world's richest regions for which other metal?

Answer

Gold is the main source of income (second-largest producer in Colombia); the San Juan River valley is one of the richest platinum regions in the world.

Q9. What is the capital of Chocó, founded in 1648 under the name Citará?

Answer

Quibdó (renamed San Francisco de Quibdó in 1690; departmental capital since 1828).

Q10. What is the highest mountain in Colombia, and in which range does it stand?

Answer

Pico Cristóbal Colón, in the Santa Marta Range (Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta) — the first-preference answer, ahead of the near-equal Pico Simón Bolívar beside it.

Q11. Pico Bolívar is the highest peak of which country, and in which range?

Answer

Venezuela — it stands in the Mérida Range. Do not confuse it with Colombia's Pico Simón Bolívar.

Q12. The Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta holds the Teyuna Archaeological Park. What is Teyuna better known as, and what superlative does the range itself hold?

Answer

Teyuna is the "Ciudad Perdida" (Lost City). The Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta is the tallest seaside (coastal) mountain system in the world and contains Colombia's two largest mountains.

Chile — Study Guide

From Ram's supplement readings (Oct 2026)

Study notes

  • National parks system — the frame: Chile protects its landscapes through an extensive national parks system administered by CONAF (the national forestry corporation). The parks run the full length of the country, from the Atacama Desert in the north to Patagonia in the far south.
  • Torres del Paine: Chile's most famous national park, in the Magallanes region of Patagonia — granite towers, lakes and steppe. It is the flagship park of the system and a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve.
  • Vicente Pérez Rosales: Chile's oldest national park (created in 1926), in the Los Lagos region, protecting the Todos los Santos Lake and Petrohué area near the Osorno volcano.
  • Easter Island (Rapa Nui) — location: A Chilean island in the southeastern Pacific, roughly 3,500 km west of mainland Chile — one of the most remote inhabited islands on Earth. It is administered as part of the Valparaíso Region.
  • Easter Island — the moai: The island is world-famous for the moai, the giant stone statues carved by the Rapa Nui people. Rapa Nui National Park protects much of the island and is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
  • Chiloé Island — location: The largest island of the Chiloé Archipelago, lying off the coast of southern Chile in the Los Lagos Region, separated from the mainland by the Chacao Channel and the Gulf of Ancud.
  • Chiloé — what it is known for: Its wooden churches (a UNESCO World Heritage group), its palafitos — colourful stilt houses built over the water — and its wet, green, maritime climate, very different from the dry north of Chile. Chiloé is also a centre of origin of the potato.
  • Isla Navarino — the far south: An island in the Magallanes region south of the Beagle Channel, at the southern tip of the Americas. Its town, Puerto Williams, is among the southernmost settlements in the world; the Dientes de Navarino mountains are a classic trekking circuit.
  • Administrative regions: Chile is divided into 16 first-order regions, running from Arica y Parinacota in the far north to Magallanes in the far south, with the capital, Santiago, in the Metropolitan Region. Ram's set includes the administrative map — learn the north-to-south order of the regions.
  • The shape that organises everything: Chile is an extremely long, narrow strip between the Andes and the Pacific — so its parks, islands and regions are best learned as a north-to-south sequence: Atacama north, lake district centre-south, Chiloé, then the fjords and Navarino at the bottom of the world.

Traps & confusables

  • Easter Island is Chilean but nowhere near Chile: It sits some 3,500 km out in the Pacific — a favourite "which country administers this island?" trap.
  • Chiloé vs Navarino: Both are southern Chilean islands, but Chiloé is in the Los Lagos lake-district zone (churches, palafitos, potatoes), while Navarino is far below it at the Beagle Channel (Puerto Williams, end-of-the-world trekking).
  • Torres del Paine vs the lake district parks: Torres del Paine is deep Patagonia (Magallanes); Vicente Pérez Rosales, the oldest park, is in Los Lagos near Osorno volcano — do not swap their regions.

Self-test

Q1. Which Chilean national park in Patagonia, famous for its granite towers, is the flagship of the country's parks system?

Answer

Torres del Paine National Park, in the Magallanes region.

Q2. Which body administers Chile's national parks system?

Answer

CONAF, the national forestry corporation.

Q3. Easter Island belongs to which country, and roughly how far west of that country's mainland does it lie?

Answer

Chile — about 3,500 km west of mainland Chile in the southeastern Pacific.

Q4. What are the giant stone statues of Easter Island called, and what is the island's Polynesian name?

Answer

The moai; the island is Rapa Nui (Rapa Nui National Park is a UNESCO World Heritage Site).

Q5. Which large island off the Los Lagos coast is known for wooden UNESCO-listed churches and palafito stilt houses?

Answer

Chiloé Island, the largest island of the Chiloé Archipelago.

Q6. Which island south of the Beagle Channel holds Puerto Williams, one of the southernmost settlements in the world?

Answer

Isla Navarino, in the Magallanes region at the southern tip of the Americas.

Q7. Into how many first-order regions is Chile divided, and which region is the farthest south?

Answer

16 regions; Magallanes is the southernmost (Arica y Parinacota is the northernmost).

Q8. What is Chile's oldest national park, and in which region is it found?

Answer

Vicente Pérez Rosales National Park (created 1926), in the Los Lagos region.

Peru — Study Guide

From Ram's supplement readings (Oct 2026)

Study notes

  • Mount Mismi — the headline fact: Nevado Mismi, a snowcapped Andean peak in southern Peru (Arequipa region), is the mountain whose glacial streams form the most distant source of the Amazon River. A stream on its slopes, the Quebrada Carhuasanta, feeds the Apurímac and so the Amazon system.
  • Volcán Misti — the headline fact: El Misti is an active stratovolcano, about 5,822 m high, whose near-perfect cone towers directly over the city of Arequipa. It is the symbol of Arequipa and one of Peru's best-known volcanoes.
  • Why the pair exists in Ram's set: Mismi and Misti are two different mountains with almost identical names, both in the Arequipa area of southern Peru. The set is built around separating them: one is a river source, the other is a volcano over a city.
  • Manu National Park — location: A vast protected area in southeastern Peru (Madre de Dios and Cusco regions), running from Andean highlands down into Amazon lowland rainforest.
  • Manu — why it matters: Manu is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the most biologically diverse places on Earth, famous for intact Amazon rainforest wildlife — jaguars, giant otters, macaws and hundreds of bird species — in a park with very restricted access.
  • Sechura Bay — location: A bay on Peru's arid northern coast, in the Piura region, at the edge of the Sechura Desert — the great coastal desert strip of the far north.
  • The Humboldt Current: The cold Humboldt (Peru) Current flows northward along Peru's coast. It chills and dries the coastal climate — one reason the Sechura coast is desert — while its nutrient-rich upwelling supports one of the world's great anchoveta fisheries.
  • The Peru coast pattern: Peru's Pacific edge is a study in contrasts: cold current, coastal desert and rich fishing waters offshore — Sechura Bay is the northern anchor of that pattern.

Traps & confusables

  • Mismi vs Misti — the core trap: Mismi = source of the Amazon (a snow peak whose meltwater starts the river). Misti = the volcano over Arequipa city. If the clue mentions the Amazon's source, answer Mismi; if it mentions a volcano or Arequipa's skyline, answer Misti.
  • Amazon source ≠ highest peak: Mismi is famous for hydrology, not height. Do not promote it into a "highest mountain" answer — and do not hand its Amazon-source fact to Misti.
  • Manu is Amazonian, not coastal: Manu sits inland in the southeast (Madre de Dios/Cusco). Sechura Bay is the coastal item in this set — keep the park and the bay in different mental zones.

Self-test

Q1. Which Peruvian mountain's glacial streams form the most distant source of the Amazon River?

Answer

Mount (Nevado) Mismi, in the Arequipa region of southern Peru.

Q2. Which active stratovolcano's perfect cone towers over the city of Arequipa?

Answer

Volcán Misti (El Misti), about 5,822 m high.

Q3. A clue says: "This Arequipa-area peak gives rise to the Amazon." Do you answer Mismi or Misti?

Answer

Mismi. Misti is the volcano overlooking the city — the similar names are the trap.

Q4. Manu National Park spans highlands and lowland rainforest in which two Peruvian regions?

Answer

Madre de Dios and Cusco, in southeastern Peru.

Q5. Why is Manu National Park world-famous among conservation scientists?

Answer

It is one of the most biologically diverse places on Earth — a UNESCO World Heritage Site protecting largely intact Amazon rainforest and its wildlife, with very restricted access.

Q6. Sechura Bay lies at the edge of which desert, on Peru's northern coast?

Answer

The Sechura Desert (Piura region).

Q7. Which cold ocean current dries Peru's coast while feeding its great anchoveta fishery?

Answer

The Humboldt (Peru) Current, flowing northward along the coast.

Q8. The stream on Mismi's slopes that starts the Amazon system is the Quebrada Carhuasanta. Into which river system does it ultimately feed?

Answer

It feeds the Apurímac, one of the headwater rivers of the Amazon.

Bolivia — Study Guide

From Ram's supplement readings (Oct 2026)

Study notes

  • Territory & history — the shrinking map: Bolivia has lost about half its original territory over roughly 200 years of independence, driven by fights over valuable resources — rubber, guano (nitrates) and oil — compounded by internal strife and poor governance.
  • Territory & history — Acre to Brazil: Brazilian rubber settlers flooded into Acre; after they expelled Bolivian officials and proclaimed an independent Republic of Acre (1899), the Treaty of Petrópolis (1903) ceded Acre to Brazil in exchange for £2 million and a promised rail link to Porto Velho, giving Bolivian trade an Atlantic outlet via the Amazon. Acre became a Brazilian state in 1962 and is nicknamed the "Latex State."
  • Territory & history — an earlier slice: Under dictator Mariano Melgarejo, the Treaty of Ayacucho (1867) had already traded a rectilinear slice of Bolivia's northernmost territory to Brazil — legend says in part for a magnificent white horse, with the border drawn in a hoof-like shape.
  • Territory & history — loss of the coast: In the War of the Pacific (1879–1883, the "Guano War") Chile defeated Bolivia and Peru, taking Peru's Tarapacá and Bolivia's Litoral department; the ironically named Treaty of Peace and Friendship (1904) cemented the loss and left Bolivia landlocked. Peru signed out in 1883, Bolivia by truce in 1884.
  • Territory & history — the living wound: Bolivia still marks the Día del Mar ("Sea Day") every March 23 — the anniversary of hero Eduardo Abaroa's death in 1879 — elects a "Miss Litoral" for its nonexistent coastal province, and maintains a navy that mainly sails Lake Titicaca. Chile and Bolivia still lack full diplomatic relations.
  • Territory & history — the Chaco War: Bolivia fought Paraguay over the Gran Chaco (1932–1935), then thought to be rich in petroleum — a proxy fight between Standard Oil (backing Bolivia) and Shell (backing Paraguay). Latin America's bloodiest war (~100,000 dead) ended with the Treaty of Buenos Aires (1938), awarding two-thirds of the Chaco to Paraguay — yet the oil and gas later found lay in the Bolivian third, giving Bolivia South America's second-largest natural gas reserves.
  • Territory & history — smaller cessions: Bolivia granted the Puna de Atacama and Chaco Central to Argentina (1889), relinquished northern territories to Peru (1903), and lost a small slice of Mato Grosso on its eastern border to Brazil (1928).
  • Territory & history — Upper Peru: Modern Bolivia was colonial "Upper Peru," named in honour of Simón Bolívar, who served as its president for four months in 1825; a bee question asking for the "last region to gain independence from Spain" wants "Upper Peru" = Bolivia (see Traps).
  • Lake Titicaca basin — Copacabana: Copacabana is a holy town on a Titicaca peninsula that juts into the lake but is connected to the mainland on the Peruvian side — reaching it from Bolivia means crossing the lake or passing through Peru. Once sacred to the Incas, it holds the revered statue of La Virgen de la Candelaria, patron saint of Bolivia, and is the best Bolivian base for Isla del Sol.
  • Lake Titicaca basin — two lakes, one river: On the Altiplano between the western and eastern central Andes, freshwater Lake Titicaca (north) drains via the Desaguadero River into saline Lake Poopó (south); Poopó is a terminal lake with no outflow — water leaves only by evaporation — so it can shrink almost dry, a climate-change concern given the nearby Salar de Uyuni salt pans.
  • Lake Titicaca basin — which Copacabana came first: Rio de Janeiro's famous Copacabana beach was named after the Bolivian town, by grateful Brazilian fishermen saved from a storm by the Virgin of Copacabana.
  • Cities — La Paz: La Paz, the world's highest capital at about 12,000 feet, is the seat of government and spills down the slopes of a steep valley — too steep for subways — so an aerial cable-car system is being built as mass-transit backbone, a world first at that scale.
  • Cities — El Alto: El Alto sits above La Paz on the high plateau: poorer, younger, more indigenous, where Aymara is heard at least as often as Spanish, while La Paz below is the older, more Western seat of "old money" — geography and rank stand in inverse relation.
  • Parks & regions — Noel Kempff Mercado NP: One of the largest and most intact parks in the Amazon Basin and one of the most biologically diverse areas in the world; a UNESCO Natural Heritage site in Bolivia's Amazon region.
  • Parks & regions — Iténez-Mamoré corridor: This binational conservation corridor runs along the Bolivian-Brazilian border on the Iténez and Mamoré Rivers, sheltering great fish diversity, giant river otters, river dolphins and birds; the Iténez Protected Area (1,389,025 ha) lies in Beni Department (Magdalena and Baures, Iténez Province).
  • Parks & regions — Gran Chaco (Bolivian side): Kaa-Iya del Gran Chaco and the Chaco lowlands form Bolivia's dry southeast — the same Gran Chaco fought over in the Chaco War (see Territory & history).
  • Andes & culture — Tiwanaku: Near Titicaca's southern shore, Tiwanaku (Tiahuanaco) was the capital of a pre-Inca civilization dominating the Andes c. 500–900 AD (the culture dawned c. 600 BC; the city flourished from c. 700 AD, ~20,000 inhabitants, faded by 1200 AD). Its Akapana pyramid and the Kalasasaya temple — with the single-block, ~44-ton Puerta del Sol (Gateway of the Sun), possibly a calendar — used stones hauled from quarries as far as the Copacabana peninsula, 40 km away.
  • Andes & culture — Inca view of Tiwanaku: The Incas believed Tiwanaku was built by the god Viracocha, who rose from Lake Titicaca and created the first humans there; each June 21 (southern winter solstice) the Aymara New Year (Machaj Mara) is celebrated at the ruins as sunrise shines through the temple entrance.
  • Andes & culture — quinoa: Quinoa originated around Lake Titicaca in Peru and Bolivia, was domesticated some 3,000–5,000 years BCE, and was a pre-Columbian staple later displaced by Spanish-introduced cereals; the shores of Lake Titicaca still hold its greatest genetic diversity. On Bolivia's proposal, the FAO declared 2013 the International Year of Quinoa.
  • Andes & culture — the Che Guevara Trail: The Ruta del Che runs Santa Cruz – Vallegrande – Pucará – La Higuera: Che Guevara was captured and executed at La Higuera (behind a schoolhouse), his body flown to Vallegrande for identification, and Vallegrande became his burial place.
  • Andes & culture — independence battles: Boyacá (Aug 7, 1819, in present-day Colombia) was Bolívar's decisive victory liberating New Granada; Junín (Aug 6, 1824, at Lake Junín in Peru, Bolívar and Sucre, fought largely with lances and swords) set the stage for Ayacucho (Dec 9, 1824), where Sucre's victory ended Spanish rule in South America.

Traps & confusables

  • "Last to gain independence" depends on framing: if the question says Upper Peru, the answer is Bolivia; if it keys on the Battle of Junín (Aug 6, 1824 — Junín is a Peruvian region, by Lake Junín), the answer is Peru. Both framings are "correct" in their sources — read the clue.
  • Two landlocked countries in the Americas: Bolivia and its neighbour Paraguay — don't answer "Bolivia is the only one."
  • Titicaca vs Poopó: Titicaca is the large freshwater lake in the north of the Altiplano; Poopó is the smaller, saline, terminal lake to the south — the Desaguadero flows Titicaca → Poopó, not the reverse.
  • Copacabana order: the Bolivian lakeside town is the original; Rio's beach took the name from it, not the other way round — and the Bolivian Copacabana peninsula is attached to the Peruvian mainland.
  • Which treaty, which loss: Petrópolis (1903) = Acre to Brazil; Peace and Friendship (1904) = Litoral/coast to Chile; Buenos Aires (1938) = most of the Chaco to Paraguay; Ayacucho (1867) = the earlier northern slice to Brazil.
  • Tiwanaku dates: the civilization's height is c. 500–900 AD, but its roots reach back to c. 600 BC and the city survived in ruins into Inca times — it is pre-Inca, not Inca-built; the Incas credited Viracocha.

Self-test

Q1. Which treaty ceded Bolivia's Acre territory to Brazil, and what did Bolivia receive in return?

Answer

The Treaty of Petrópolis (1903). Bolivia received £2 million and the promise of a rail link to Porto Velho, giving its trade an Atlantic outlet via the Amazon River.

Q2. What made the loss of Litoral in 1904 so traumatic, and which war caused it?

Answer

It left Bolivia landlocked — its most traumatic defeat. The loss resulted from the War of the Pacific (1879–1883), fought with Chile over the guano/nitrate-rich Atacama; the Treaty of Peace and Friendship (1904) cemented it.

Q3. What does Bolivia commemorate every March 23, and what else keeps the memory of its lost coast alive?

Answer

The Día del Mar ("Sea Day"), on the anniversary of Eduardo Abaroa's death in 1879. Bolivia also elects a "Miss Litoral" for its nonexistent coastal province and maintains a navy that mainly sails Lake Titicaca.

Q4. In the Chaco War, which country won most of the Gran Chaco — and where was the oil and gas actually found?

Answer

Paraguay won two-thirds of the Chaco under the Treaty of Buenos Aires (1938). Ironically, the oil and gas later discovered were in the third that remained Bolivian — giving Bolivia South America's second-largest natural gas reserves.

Q5. Why is reaching Copacabana from the rest of Bolivia geographically awkward?

Answer

Copacabana sits on a peninsula extending into Lake Titicaca that is connected to the mainland on the Peruvian side — so travellers from Bolivia must either cross the lake or pass through Peruvian territory.

Q6. How are Lake Titicaca and Lake Poopó connected, and why does Poopó sometimes nearly dry up?

Answer

The Desaguadero River carries water from freshwater Titicaca (north) to saline Poopó (south). Poopó is a terminal lake with no outflow — water leaves only by evaporation — so in dry periods, or when the river silts up, it can become extremely shallow.

Q7. Which is the world's highest capital, how high is it, and why is it building cable cars as mass transit?

Answer

La Paz, at about 12,000 feet above sea level. It spills down a steep valley where subways or trains are impractical and buses gridlock, so an aerial cable-car system serves as the transit backbone linking it with El Alto.

Q8. How do La Paz and El Alto contrast in position, population and language?

Answer

La Paz lies below in a valley — the seat of government, older and more Western, speaking Spanish. El Alto sits above on the plateau — poorer, younger, more indigenous, where Aymara is heard at least as often as Spanish.

Q9. What is Tiwanaku, when did its civilization dominate the Andes, and what is its most famous structure?

Answer

Tiwanaku (Tiahuanaco), near Lake Titicaca's southern shore, was the capital of a pre-Inca civilization that dominated the Andean region c. 500–900 AD. Its most famous structure is the Puerta del Sol (Gateway of the Sun) in the Kalasasaya temple — a single andesite block of at least 44 tons, possibly used as a calendar.

Q10. What did the Incas believe about Tiwanaku, and what festival is held there on June 21?

Answer

The Incas believed the god Viracocha built it — rising from Lake Titicaca, he created the first humans there. On June 21, the southern winter solstice, the Aymara New Year (Machaj Mara) is celebrated as sunrise shines through the temple entrance.

Q11. Where did quinoa originate, and what international honour did it receive in 2013?

Answer

Quinoa originated in the area surrounding Lake Titicaca in Peru and Bolivia, where its greatest genetic diversity is still found. On Bolivia's proposal, the FAO declared 2013 the International Year of Quinoa.

Q12. Where was Che Guevara captured and executed, and where was he buried?

Answer

He was captured and executed at the village of La Higuera (behind a schoolhouse) on the Ruta del Che; his body was flown to Vallegrande for identification, and Vallegrande became his burial place.

Q13. Put Boyacá, Junín and Ayacucho in order with their dates, and say what each achieved.

Answer

Boyacá — Aug 7, 1819: Bolívar's decisive victory liberating New Granada (now Colombia). Junín — Aug 6, 1824: Bolívar and Sucre routed the Spanish at Lake Junín in Peru. Ayacucho — Dec 9, 1824: Sucre's victory that effectively ended Spanish rule in South America.

Q14. A bee question says "Upper Peru was the last region to gain independence from Spain. What is its present-day name?" — and how would the answer change if the clue were the Battle of Junín?

Answer

"Upper Peru" = Bolivia. But if the framing keys on Junín (Aug 6, 1824 — Junín is a region of Peru, by Lake Junín), the intended answer is Peru.

Argentina — Study Guide

From Ram's supplement readings (Oct 2026)

Study notes

  • The Gran Chaco — the set's core: Argentina's Chaco is part of the Gran Chaco lowland shared with Bolivia and Paraguay (and a small part of Brazil); within Argentina it spans provinces including Formosa, Chaco, Salta, Jujuy, Santiago del Estero, Tucumán, Santa Fe, Córdoba, Catamarca and La Rioja.
  • Three agro-ecological zones: the Chaco divides into (1) the humid to sub-humid (Oriental/eastern) Chaco, (2) the arid and semi-arid Chaco, growing drier from east to west, and (3) the montane or highland zone in the west, where hills catch moist Atlantic air and rainfall is higher than on the lowlands.
  • Humid (Oriental) Chaco: a parkland of quebracho colorado forest patches alternating with open grassland; flat relief plus heavy seasonal rain creates numerous shallow lagoons and wetlands called esteros, with palm groves of Copernicia alba on swampy, salt-accumulating ground.
  • Arid Chaco: flat land dominated by xerophyte (drought-adapted) forest — quebracho (Schinopsis lorentzii), algarrobo (Prosopis species) and abundant cacti, including tree cacti; useful forage exists mainly in man-made clearings, and saline soils support salt-tolerant grasses and Atriplex.
  • Quebracho — the signature tree: quebracho forests (quebrachales) define both wet and dry Chaco; Schinopsis species anchor the humid east (S. balansae) and the arid west (S. lorentzii), alongside algarrobales of Prosopis (algarrobo) trees.
  • Agriculture — northeast: Formosa and Chaco provinces (over 170,000 km², about 32% of the Argentinean Chaco) are the cotton heartland — cotton covers ~70% of Argentina's total cotton area — with sunflower, sorghum and fast-growing soybean; livestock raising takes ~68% of land use there, crops ~16%.
  • Agriculture — northwest & southwest: Salta and Jujuy mix irrigated sugar cane, tobacco, soybean and citrus across many ecological zones; Tucumán's farming rests heavily on sugar (~50% of its crops); Catamarca and La Rioja are arid (rainfall under 400 mm/yr), where water scarcity limits everything and irrigation is small-scale.
  • Degradation: across the Chaco, forest clearing, monoculture, overgrazing and poor irrigation have caused severe erosion, salinization and loss of native species — only about 20% of the forest standing in 1914 remains in Santiago del Estero/Tucumán; large native animals (guanaco, tapir, jaguar, giant armadillo) have almost disappeared from settled areas.
  • Río de la Plata basin (set topic): the set's maps cover the Río de la Plata Basin — the great estuary system fed by the Paraná and Uruguay Rivers, draining the Chaco and Pampas lowlands between Argentina and Uruguay.
  • Campos region (set topic): the Campos is the grassland region spanning southern Brazil, Paraguay, northeastern Argentina and Uruguay — the Plata basin's pastoral borderland covered by the set's regional document.
  • Itaipu Dam (set topic): the set includes the Itaipu map — the giant binational hydroelectric dam on the Paraná River between Brazil and Paraguay, at the northern edge of Argentina's Misiones frontier.
  • Aconcagua correction (set topic): Ram's set carries a correction note on Aconcagua — Argentina's (and the Americas') highest peak, in the Mendoza Andes — a reminder to double-check peak heights/locations against his corrected version before a bee.

Traps & confusables

  • Gran Chaco ≠ Argentine only: the Gran Chaco is shared by Argentina, Bolivia and Paraguay (plus a sliver of Brazil) — and Bolivia's share was the Chaco War battleground; Argentina's Chaco is its southern portion.
  • Wet east vs dry west: the Chaco gets more arid from east to west — the humid parkland-and-esteros east (Formosa/Chaco provinces) is the opposite end from the cactus-and-xerophyte arid west (Catamarca/La Rioja, under 400 mm of rain).
  • Two quebrachos: Schinopsis balansae typifies the humid eastern quebrachales; Schinopsis lorentzii the arid western ones — same genus, different zones.
  • Esteros are wetlands, not rivers: esteros are the shallow lagoons/bogs that form on the flat, flood-prone humid Chaco — a landform term worth recognizing on its own.
  • Itaipu's owners: Itaipu is Brazil–Paraguay, on the Paraná — not an Argentine dam, even though it appears in an Argentina set (it anchors the basin's upstream end near Misiones).

Self-test

Q1. Which four countries share the Gran Chaco, and which Argentine provinces in the set's text lie within it?

Answer

Argentina, Bolivia, Paraguay and (a small part of) Brazil. Argentine provinces include Formosa, Chaco, Salta, Jujuy, Santiago del Estero, Tucumán, Santa Fe, Córdoba, Catamarca and La Rioja.

Q2. Name the Chaco's three agro-ecological zones and the direction of the rainfall gradient.

Answer

The humid to sub-humid (Oriental) Chaco; the arid and semi-arid Chaco; and the montane/highland zone. Rainfall decreases from east to west — the land grows increasingly arid westward.

Q3. What are esteros, and in which Chaco zone do they form?

Answer

Esteros are shallow lagoons and bogs. They form in the humid (Oriental) Chaco, where flat relief and heavy seasonal rainfall leave standing water across the parkland.

Q4. What vegetation defines the humid Chaco, and which palm grows on its swampy, salty ground?

Answer

A parkland of quebracho colorado (Schinopsis) forest patches alternating with open grassland. The palm is Copernicia alba, found in palm groves (palmares) under swamp conditions with salt accumulation.

Q5. How does the arid Chaco's forest differ from the humid Chaco's, and which trees and plants typify it?

Answer

It is a less dense forest of xerophytes (drought-adapted plants): quebracho (Schinopsis lorentzii), algarrobo (Prosopis species) and many cacti, including tree cacti.

Q6. Which two provinces form Argentina's cotton heartland, and roughly what share of the country's cotton area do they hold?

Answer

Formosa and Chaco provinces — cotton there represents about 70% of the total area of the crop in Argentina.

Q7. What environmental damage does the Chaco text document, and what are its main causes?

Answer

Widespread soil erosion, salinization, forest loss (only ~20% of the 1914 forest remains around Santiago del Estero/Tucumán) and near-disappearance of large native wildlife. Causes: indiscriminate logging and clearing, cereal monoculture, overgrazing and poor irrigation/drainage.

Q8. Which river system does the set's Plata basin material cover, and which two countries operate the Itaipu Dam?

Answer

The Río de la Plata Basin — the estuary system fed by the Paraná and Uruguay Rivers between Argentina and Uruguay. Itaipu, on the Paraná River, is operated by Brazil and Paraguay.

Venezuela — Study Guide (Readings)

From Ram's extra reading set (Sep 2026)

Study notes

  • Angel Falls — the tepui: Auyán-tepui is a 435-square-mile, heart-shaped table mountain in Canaima National Park in the Gran Sabana of southeastern Venezuela. In the Pemón language, auyán means "devil" and tepui means "house," hence "Devil's House" (also translated "Devil's Mountain").
  • Angel Falls — the discovery: American aviator Jimmie Angel, working as a guide in the Gran Sabana for the Santa Ana Mining Company of Tulsa, Oklahoma, first saw the falls on a solo flight on 14 November 1933. His companions, mining official D. H. Curry and co-pilot José Cardona, left soon after and refused to believe his story of a "mile-high waterfall."
  • Angel Falls — the drop: At 979 metres (3,212 feet) it is the world's highest waterfall; its other names are Salto Ángel and Kerepakupai Merú. A stream from the top of Auyán-tepui plunges into Devil's Canyon below, and its water eventually joins the Churún River downstream.
  • Angel Falls — not the Churún: The Churún River is a separate watercourse from the falls' stream. It also rises on Auyán-tepui and carves the Devil's Canyon (Cañón del Diablo, or Churún Canyon); Churún Falls (Churún Merú) is a different waterfall on that river, farther southwest in the canyon.
  • Guri Dam — identity: Also called the Raúl Leoni Dam and the Simón Bolívar Hydroelectric Power Station, it is a concrete gravity dam on the Caroní River in Bolívar State, about sixty miles south of the Caroní–Orinoco junction in Necuima Canyon. It stands 156 metres high and 1,176 metres long, holds back 110 million acre-feet of water (8th largest dam by retention), and was built in two stages, 1963–1968 and to 1986.
  • Guri Dam — power: Venezuela's largest source of hydroelectric power: two machine rooms with ten generators each, about 10 million kW of capacity. It generates roughly 70% of Venezuela's electricity, saving some 300,000 barrels of oil a day and preventing about 20 million tons of CO₂ a year; surplus electricity is exported to Colombia and Brazil.
  • Guri Dam — the lake: The reservoir, Guri Lake, covers about 4,000 sq. km — the second-largest lake in Venezuela — and extends 80 km upstream. Its watershed covers 92,170 sq. km (10% of the country), averages about 2,900 mm of rain a year, and is 67% forested, though poor, erosion-prone soils mean only about 10% is farmed.
  • Guri Dam — the costs: Building it flooded about 4,300 sq. km of rainforest and displaced Pemón, Yekuana and Karina indigenous communities. The flooded islands it created unbalanced predator–prey systems (with predators gone, howler monkey populations exploded), and rotting submerged vegetation emits greenhouse gases.
  • Guri Dam — over-reliance: In April 2008, forest fires shut the dam down and about half of Venezuela lost power for a day, prompting a government plan under President Chávez to expand 42 other renewable energy sources. A renovation project begun in 2000 aims to extend the plant's operations by 30 years.
  • Guri Dam — future threats: Large gold and diamond mining operations upstream load the catchment with sediment, and forest fires and deforestation in the watershed — which quickly turns forest into savannah — alter the hydrologic cycle that supplies the dam's runoff.
  • Pacaraima Mountains: A chain shared by Venezuela, Brazil and Guyana, extending about 250 miles (400 km) east–west. It forms the drainage divide between the Orinoco Valley to the north and the Amazon Basin to the south. Mount Roraima (9,094 feet / 2,772 metres) is its highest point; rivers pouring off the plateau edges form spectacular falls such as Kaieteur Falls in Guyana, and the range is noted for unique wildlife such as the Golden Frog.
  • Parima Mountains: An outlying range of the Guiana Highlands in southern Venezuela and northern Brazil, running about 200 miles (320 km) south-southeast and separating the two countries. Its largely unexplored peaks reach about 5,000 feet (1,500 metres); the headwaters of the Orinoco rise on its western flanks and those of the Branco River descend its eastern slopes, and it connects with the Pacaraima Mountains in the northeast.
  • The Guiana Shield: The Guianas sit on this Precambrian geological formation, bounded by Brazil to the south and east, Venezuela to the west and the Atlantic Ocean to the north.
  • The five Guianas: British Guiana became Guyana (independent 1966); Spanish Guiana is present-day southeastern Venezuela; French Guiana (Guyane) remains an overseas region and department of France; Dutch Guiana became Suriname (independent 1975); Portuguese Guiana became the Brazilian state of Amapá.

Self-test

Q1. What do the Pemón words auyán and tepui mean?

Answer

Auyán means "devil" and tepui means "house" (a table mountain) — so Auyán-tepui is the "Devil's House."

Q2. When did Jimmie Angel first see Angel Falls, and what was he doing in the Gran Sabana?

Answer

On 14 November 1933, on a solo flight into Devil's Canyon. He was working as an aviator guide in the Gran Sabana for the Santa Ana Mining Company of Tulsa, Oklahoma.

Q3. How is Angel Falls different from Churún Falls (Churún Merú)?

Answer

They are separate waterfalls. Angel Falls is formed by a stream from the top of Auyán-tepui plunging into Devil's Canyon; Churún Merú is a different waterfall on the Churún River itself, farther southwest in the same canyon.

Q4. How high is Angel Falls?

Answer

979 metres (3,212 feet) — the highest waterfall in the world.

Q5. On which river, and in which state, is the Guri Dam built?

Answer

On the Caroní River in Bolívar State, about sixty miles south of where the Caroní meets the Orinoco.

Q6. About what share of Venezuela's electricity does the Guri Dam produce, and what does that save?

Answer

About 70%. It saves roughly 300,000 barrels of oil a day (and avoids about 20 million tons of CO₂ a year).

Q7. What happened to Venezuela's power supply in April 2008?

Answer

Forest fires shut down the Guri Dam, and about half the country was blacked out for a day — a warning about how heavily Venezuela relies on the one dam.

Q8. Name two negative effects of building the Guri Dam.

Answer

Any two: it flooded about 4,300 sq. km of rainforest and its ecosystem; it displaced Pemón, Yekuana and Karina indigenous communities; its flooded islands unbalanced predator–prey populations (e.g., the howler monkey explosion); rotting flooded vegetation emits greenhouse gases.

Q9. What drainage divide do the Pacaraima Mountains form, and what is their highest point?

Answer

They divide the Orinoco Valley (north) from the Amazon Basin (south). Their highest point is Mount Roraima, 9,094 feet (2,772 metres).

Q10. Which of the five colonial Guianas is still an overseas department of France today?

Answer

French Guiana (Guyane).

Brazil — Study Guide

From Ram's Brazil advanced readings (Sep 2026)

Study notes

  • Rivers & the Amazon — the basin: Brazil contains most of the Amazon River basin — the world's largest river system and its most extensive virgin rainforest. Almost half of Brazil's territory is Amazon basin; about 65% of the rainforest lies in Brazil, and the forest overall covers some 40% of South America across nine countries.
  • Rivers & the Amazon — Belém: Belém, capital and port of Pará state, is sometimes itself called "Pará." It stands on Guajará Bay, part of the vast Amazon delta, near the mouth of the Guamá River, about 130 km (80 miles) up the Pará River from the Atlantic Ocean.
  • Rivers & the Amazon — the Pará River: This delta channel is the estuary of the Tocantins River. It passes south and east of Marajó Island, carries a small part of the Amazon's discharge to the Atlantic off Cape Maguarinho, and receives the massive Tocantins from the south. Its full 320 km (200 miles) is navigable. Its tidal bore — a strong tide that pushes upriver against the current, a true tidal wave and a "positive surge" — reaches 12 feet (4 m).
  • Rivers & the Amazon — São Francisco River: Rises at about 730 m on the Serra da Canastra in southwestern Minas Gerais, then flows over 1,000 miles north across Minas Gerais and Bahia, through the Sobradinho Reservoir, to the twin cities of Juazeiro and Petrolina. Below them a 300-mile rapids stretch forms the Bahia–Pernambuco border and culminates in Paulo Afonso Falls (total drop about 275 feet); the lower river then forms the Sergipe–Alagoas border before reaching the Atlantic northeast of Aracaju.
  • Rivers & the Amazon — Paraná River: Formed by the junction of the Grande and Paranaíba rivers in south-central Brazil, the Paraná is 4,695 km long — the 10th longest river in the world — and flows into the Río de la Plata in Argentina. The Upper Paraná is noted for spectacular waterfalls and exceptional freshwater fish diversity and endemism.
  • Rivers & the Amazon — Pantanal: The Paraguay and Paraná rivers flood the Pantanal, the world's largest wetland. The Pantanal Conservation Area (Mato Grosso and Mato Grosso do Sul, near the Bolivian border) protects the headwaters of the Cuiabá and Paraguay river systems.
  • Rivers & the Amazon — deforestation: Cattle ranching is the leading cause of Amazon deforestation; the cattle herd more than doubled between 1990 and 2002, and almost 700,000 km² of Brazilian Amazon were cleared between 1970 and 2007. Soybean farming adds pressure by pushing other farmers deeper into the forest.
  • Islands — Marajó: Marajó Island sits in the Amazon delta in northeastern Pará state; the Pará River passes to its south and east on its way to the Atlantic.
  • Islands — Fernando de Noronha: A volcanic archipelago about 340 km off the Brazilian coast, formed by the peaks of a submerged mountain chain. Nearly 70% of the main island plus 21 smaller islands and islets form a Marine National Park. It belongs to the state of Pernambuco, was sighted by Amerigo Vespucci in 1503, takes its name from Fernão de Loronha (granted the islands in 1504), served as a penal colony, and was visited by Darwin on the Beagle.
  • Islands — Atol das Rocas: The only atoll in the South Atlantic Ocean, lying about 150 km west of Fernando de Noronha. It was Brazil's first marine biological reserve (first Brazilian Marine Protected Area, 1979), is built mainly by vermetids and coralline algae rather than coral, and belongs to Rio Grande do Norte state — not Pernambuco. Only researchers may visit.
  • Islands — the two reserves together: Fernando de Noronha and Atol das Rocas form one World Heritage site. Their rich waters are vital breeding and feeding grounds for tuna, shark, turtles and marine mammals, hold the Western Atlantic's largest concentration of tropical seabirds, and Baía dos Golfinhos has an exceptional resident dolphin population.
  • Falls & dams — Iguazu Falls: The falls (Iguaçu in Portuguese, Iguazú in Spanish) sit on the Iguazu River in southern Brazil, about 650 km from the coast. Just before the Iguazu meets the Paraná, the river plunges in 275 interlinked cataracts spread over nearly 3 km. Downriver the Iguazu forms the Brazil–Argentina (Misiones) boundary; twin national parks — Iguaçu (Brazil) and Iguazú (Argentina) — protect the falls, and the river empties into the Paraná at the Triple Frontier of Brazil, Argentina and Paraguay.
  • Falls & dams — Itaipu Dam: A binational Brazil–Paraguay gravity dam on the Upper Paraná River, completed in 1991. The numbers repeat: 18 generators, 18 years to build, $18 billion cost, a 4.8-mile-long complex generating 12,600 MW — supplying 25% of Brazil's energy and 78% of Paraguay's. Engineers diverted the Paraná through a 1.3-mile channel to build it; the American Society of Civil Engineers named it one of the Seven Wonders of the Modern World.
  • Places & heritage — Brasília: A capital created ex nihilo in the centre of the country from 1956, a landmark of town planning by Lúcio Costa and architect Oscar Niemeyer; its residential and administrative layout is often compared to the shape of a bird in flight.
  • Places & heritage — Christ the Redeemer: The statue stands on Corcovado Mountain in Rio de Janeiro, at 710 m (2,328 ft) above sea level. The statue itself is 38 m (125 ft) tall including its 8 m pedestal, with 30 m between the fingertips. Ram's bee advice: if asked the height, answer 125 feet including the pedestal.
  • Places & heritage — Salvador de Bahia: Brazil's first capital, from 1549 to 1763, where European, African and Amerindian cultures blended; from 1558 it was the first slave market in the New World, with slaves arriving to work the sugar plantations.
  • Places & heritage — Ouro Preto, Olinda, São Luís: Ouro Preto ("Black Gold") in Minas Gerais was the focal point of the 18th-century gold rush and is linked to the Baroque sculptor Aleijadinho. Olinda (Pernambuco), a Portuguese sugar town looted by the Dutch, keeps 20 Baroque churches. São Luís (Maranhão) was founded by the French, occupied by the Dutch, then ruled by Portugal, and preserves its original rectangular street plan intact.
  • Places & heritage — the two Diamantinas: The Historic Centre of Diamantina, a colonial town in Minas Gerais set among inhospitable rocky mountains, recalls the 18th-century diamond prospectors. Chapada Diamantina National Park is a different place with a similar name — in Bahia.
  • Places & heritage — Serra da Capivara: This national park in Piauí holds rock shelters decorated with cave paintings, some more than 25,000 years old — testimony to one of the oldest human communities in South America.
  • Places & heritage — music & cowboys: Signature styles: samba; choro, an instrumental style born in 1800s Rio (Ernesto Nazareth its best-known publisher); carimbó from eastern Amazonia, which evolved into lambada; and the music of capoeira, with African roots. A gaúcho is the traditional cowboy of Rio Grande do Sul — the "Texas of Brazil" — and of Argentina and Uruguay; the Northeast's cowboy is the vaqueiro, and Chile's is the huaso.
  • Regions — the five major regions: Brazil's states are grouped into five large geographic regions. The North (Acre, Rondônia, Amazonas, Pará, Tocantins, Roraima, Amapá) is the largest at 45.27% of the territory and the least inhabited, holding most of the Amazon rainforest plus iron mining at Carajás in Pará. The South (Paraná, Santa Catarina, Rio Grande do Sul) is the smallest region, heavily shaped by Italian, German and Portuguese immigration, with Brazil's highest HDI; it borders Uruguay, Argentina and Paraguay.
  • Regions — Brazil in brief: The fifth-largest country in the world (after Russia, Canada, China and the USA) at about 8.5 million km², covering almost half of South America. It borders every South American country except Chile and Ecuador. It has three time zones, its highest point is Pico da Neblina (3,014 m), most people live along the coast, and it became independent from Portugal on 7 September 1822, with Brasília as capital.
  • Regions — Amapá, the former Portuguese Guiana: The Guianas sit on the Guiana Shield: British Guiana became Guyana, Dutch Guiana became Suriname, French Guiana remains a French overseas department, Spanish Guiana is now southeastern Venezuela — and Portuguese Guiana is today the Brazilian state of Amapá.

Traps & confusables

  • Noronha vs Rocas — states: Fernando de Noronha belongs to Pernambuco, but Atol das Rocas belongs to Rio Grande do Norte. The pairing invites a swap; don't.
  • Noronha vs Rocas — position & type: Rocas lies about 150 km WEST of Noronha and is an atoll (the South Atlantic's only one); Noronha is a volcanic archipelago and the farther of the two from the mainland story — Rocas is the reef built by algae and vermetids, not true coral.
  • Three things called Pará: Pará is a state, its capital Belém is nicknamed "Pará," and the Pará River is the Tocantins-estuary channel Belém sits on — while Belém itself stands on Guajará Bay near the Guamá's mouth.
  • Itaipu is Brazil–Paraguay, not Brazil–Argentina: and the energy shares are lopsided — 78% of Paraguay's supply versus 25% of Brazil's.
  • The falls are not on the Paraná: Iguazu Falls are on the Iguazu River; the Iguazu joins the Paraná only after the falls, at the Triple Frontier. Spellings mark the language: Iguaçu (Brazil/Portuguese), Iguazú (Argentina/Spanish).
  • Diamantina vs Chapada Diamantina: the diamond-prospecting World Heritage town is in Minas Gerais; the national park of the similar name is in Bahia.
  • São Francisco's two borders: the same river borders Bahia–Pernambuco in its rapids stretch, then Sergipe–Alagoas in its lower course — after flowing north first, not south.
  • The Amazon reaches the Atlantic, not the Pacific: Belém and the Pará River are on the Atlantic side, about 130 km upriver from the ocean.
  • Gaúcho vs vaqueiro vs huaso: gaúcho = Rio Grande do Sul (and Argentina/Uruguay); vaqueiro = Brazil's Northeast; huaso = Chile.
