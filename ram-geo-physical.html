Physical Geography — Geomorphology Glossary (Distilled)

The 40 highest-value terms from Ram's 122-page landforms volume & glossary

The 40 terms

  • Abrasion — the mechanical wearing down, scraping or grinding away of a rock surface by friction from particles carried by wind, ice, running water, waves or gravity (not the same as attrition, which is fragments wearing each other down).
  • Abyssal plain — a flat underwater plain on the deep ocean floor, usually 3,000–6,000 m deep, lying between the foot of a continental rise and a mid-ocean ridge.
  • Alluvial fan — a cone-shaped deposit built where a steep, high-power channel enters a zone of reduced stream power, spreading sediment out from an apex at the channel mouth.
  • Barchan — an active crescent-shaped dune that forms where winds blow from one direction and the sand supply is limited.
  • Barrier island — a long, narrow island of sand running parallel to the coast, bordered by tidal inlets and lagoon entrances; typically 0.5–5 km wide and up to 100 km long.
  • Mesa and butte — flat-topped hills in flat-lying sedimentary rock with a resistant caprock; a butte is the smaller, narrower form, an advanced stage of mesa degradation.
  • Caldera — a large crater or depression resulting from a powerful volcanic explosion or the collapse of the central part of a volcano.
  • Cirque — a bowl-shaped depression formed by glacial erosion of bedrock (also called a corrie or cwm); often holds a small lake after the ice melts.
  • Deflation — the entrainment and transport of fine sediment from a surface deposit by the action of wind.
  • Delta — a depositional landform produced by sedimentation at and around the mouth of a river.
  • Drumlin — an elongated, streamlined mound of glacial till deposited and shaped beneath an ice sheet; its long axis points the way the ice flowed.
  • Erratic — a rock that lies on contrasting bedrock because it was transported a long distance from its origin, usually by a glacier.
  • Esker — a sinuous, winding ridge of sediment laid down in a meandering stream that flowed beneath a glacier.
  • Estuary — the seaward end of a river, opening to the sea through a typically funnel-shaped inlet, subject to tides and incursions of salt water.
  • Exfoliation — the peeling away of layers or sheets of rock from an exposed rock surface.
  • Fault — a fracture in rock, caused by tensional or compressive forces, along which there has been displacement.
  • Fjord — a deeply glaciated coastal valley that was flooded after the glacier retreated and sea level rose.
  • Floodplain — a relatively flat alluvial landform built by the present river's flow regime and subject to flooding.
  • Hanging valley — a tributary valley that joins a main (often glacially deepened) valley at a sharp break of slope, commonly leaving a waterfall.
  • Horst — an upstanding block of crust bounded by faults and uplifted by tectonic forces; the down-faulted blocks on either side are graben.
  • Inselberg — literally an "island mountain": a large residual hill rising abruptly within an eroded plain.
  • Karst — a landscape formed by the dissolution of soluble rocks such as limestone, dolomite and gypsum, characterised by underground drainage, sinkholes, dolines and caves.
  • Lagoon — a coastal stretch of shallow water almost cut off from the open sea by a barrier beach, spit or reef.
  • Loess — a predominantly wind-deposited material dominated by silt; it forms fertile, easily eroded soils.
  • Longshore drift — the movement of beach sediment along the shore by waves arriving at an angle to the coastline and the currents they generate.
  • Meander — a sinuous bend or curve in a river channel.
  • Moraine — material, and the depositional landforms it makes, produced on, within and beneath a glacier or ice sheet.
  • Ox-bow — a horseshoe-shaped, almost closed meander loop cut off from the main channel; it may be occupied by an ox-bow lake.
  • Permafrost — ground that stays below 0°C through at least two consecutive winters and the intervening summer; the surface active layer above it thaws each summer and refreezes in winter.
  • Playa — an ephemeral (temporary) lake, normally in a dryland basin; when dry it leaves a flat salt- or mud-crusted playa surface.
  • Roche moutonnée — a rock projection moulded by glacial abrasion and plucking (the quarrying of bedrock blocks by ice): gently sloping and rounded on the upglacier (stoss) side, steeper and more rugged on the downglacier side.
  • Scree — a slope accumulation of angular rock fragments produced by weathering of a free face (the deposit itself is also called talus); freeze-thaw prising fragments loose is a classic source.
  • Sinkhole — a depression formed by solution and collapse in a limestone area; dolines are the enclosed karst depressions of which sinkholes are the best-known type.
  • Spit — a finger-like ridge of beach material built above high-tide level, attached to the land at one (proximal) end and usually ending in one or more recurved hooks.
  • Stack — an isolated, steep-sided rock pillar rising from the shore or sea floor close to a cliffed coast, left behind as a headland erodes.
  • Stalactite and stalagmite — calcite deposits in limestone caves: a stalactite hangs from the roof, formed by dripping water rich in dissolved calcium carbonate; a stalagmite grows upward from the floor.
  • Swash and backwash — swash is the rapid uprush of a breaking wave up the beach face; backwash is the flow back down the beach afterwards. Their balance decides whether a beach gains or loses sediment.
  • Tarn — a small mountain lake, typically occupying a cirque basin.
  • Tombolo — a ridge or barrier of sand or shingle built above high-tide level that links a former island to the mainland, or unites two islands.
  • Yardang — a streamlined wind-erosion form with its long axis parallel to the wind — carved by wind, not deposited like a dune.

Climate Graphs & Written Work — Study Notes

From Ram's May 2026 answer sets (climate graphs, Written Tests 1-2, cadastral maps)

How to read a climate graph

  • A climate graph pairs two series: bars show monthly precipitation, the line shows monthly temperature. Read each against its own axis before saying anything else.
  • First check: the hemisphere. A temperature peak in July means Northern Hemisphere; a peak in January (with the coldest months mid-year) means Southern Hemisphere — the trap built into Ram's Darwin and Port Elizabeth set.
  • Second check: the annual temperature range. A huge range with winters far below freezing signals a continental interior (Ulaanbaatar, Moscow, Whitehorse); a small range signals maritime moderation (London, Vancouver, Reykjavik).
  • Third check: rainfall timing, not just totals. A single sharp summer spike is a monsoon signature (New Delhi, Islamabad, Manila); rain concentrated in winter with a dry summer is Mediterranean (Lisbon); near-zero rain all year is arid (Baghdad, Cairo).
  • A nearly flat temperature line is an equatorial clue — then use the level of the line: flat and hot means lowland rainforest (Manaus, Libreville); flat and cool means high altitude at the equator (Bogota, Bujumbura).
  • Highland moderation is Ram's favourite distractor pair: Mexico City and Guatemala City sit in the tropics but their elevation keeps temperatures mild, with rain concentrated in the summer months.
  • Coastal deserts break the "coast = wet" instinct: Lima records almost no rain despite sitting on the Pacific — cold offshore water stabilises the air. Always check the bar heights, not the map position.
  • Cold-climate trios are separated by range and precipitation: Sapporo has warm summers and heavy snow-season precipitation, Nuuk stays cold all year (tundra), Whitehorse has the biggest range and low precipitation (subarctic continental).
  • The method the worked answers reward: state the hemisphere, the temperature range, the wet and dry seasons, name the climate type — and only then match the station. Description first, identification second.

Written tests — the answer pattern

  • Ram's Written Test Parts 1 and 2 answer sets are worked answers keyed directly to the source material — marks go to short, exact answers, not essays.
  • Name the feature precisely: the exact place, landform or process named in the material, spelled as the source spells it.
  • Quote values with units (heights in metres, distances, temperatures, rainfall in mm) — a number without a unit is an unfinished answer.
  • Answer from the evidence given — the map, graph or text in front of you — rather than from general knowledge; the correction sets reward what can be read off the source.

What a cadastral map is — and the correction points

  • A cadastral map is a detailed graphic record of land parcels: it shows who owns what land and where the boundaries fall, with each parcel's dimensions and an identifier linking it to ownership and legal records.
  • The word comes from "cadastre" — a public record of the value, extent and ownership of land used as a basis for taxation.
  • It is not a topographic map: a cadastral map records ownership and boundaries, not relief, landforms or elevation. Confusing the two is the classic error.
  • Boundary lines on the map reflect the legal descriptions recorded in deeds, so the map's precision depends on the quality of the underlying surveys.
  • The Bureau of Land Management warning Ram's set highlights: "the ambiguous descriptions of the past are the boundary disputes of the future."
  • Main uses: property transactions, tax assessment, land administration and development planning — cadastral maps are the backbone of land administration.

Physical Geography — Self-Test

Q1. Both are winding glacial deposits — what is the difference between a drumlin and an esker?

Answer

A drumlin is a streamlined mound of glacial till shaped beneath an ice sheet; an esker is a ridge of sediment laid down by a stream flowing beneath the ice.

Q2. A tarn is a small mountain lake. In which glacial landform does it usually sit?

Answer

A cirque — the bowl-shaped depression gouged out by glacial erosion (also called a corrie or cwm).

Q3. What two conditions produce a barchan dune, and what shape does it take?

Answer

Winds blowing from one main direction and a limited sand supply; the dune is crescent-shaped.

Q4. Which hangs from a cave roof — a stalactite or a stalagmite — and what builds both?

Answer

The stalactite hangs from the roof; a stalagmite grows up from the floor. Both are calcite deposits left by water carrying dissolved calcium carbonate.

Q5. What is the difference between a spit and a tombolo?

Answer

A spit is a ridge of beach material attached to the land at one end and free at the other; a tombolo goes all the way — it links an island to the mainland (or two islands together).

Q6. On a roche moutonnée, which side is gently sloping and which is steep — and what does that tell you?

Answer

The upglacier (stoss) side is gently sloping and rounded by abrasion; the downglacier side is steeper and rugged from plucking. The asymmetry shows the direction the ice flowed.

Q7. A mesa and a butte stand in the same desert landscape. How do you tell them apart?

Answer

Both are flat-topped hills with a resistant caprock; the butte is smaller and narrower — it is a mesa worn down to a later stage.

Q8. A granite boulder sits alone on a limestone plain far from any granite. What is it called, and what does it prove?

Answer

An erratic. It proves a glacier transported it a long distance from its source rock and dropped it on contrasting bedrock when the ice melted.

Q9. Karst landscapes form in which kinds of rock, and by what process?

Answer

In soluble rocks — limestone, dolomite and gypsum — dissolved by water, producing underground drainage, sinkholes, dolines and caves.

Q10. A climate graph shows the warmest month in January and the coldest in July. What does that establish, and which two stations in Ram's sets fit it?

Answer

The station is in the Southern Hemisphere — the seasons are reversed. Darwin (Australia) and Port Elizabeth (South Africa) fit.

Q11. A city's climate graph is an almost straight temperature line around 14°C all year, yet the city sits almost on the equator. Which city in Ram's sets is it, and why is it so cool?

Answer

Bogota, Colombia. Its high-altitude Andean location cools it year-round, while the equatorial position keeps the temperature nearly constant.

Q12. Which coastal capital in Ram's sets has a climate graph with almost no rainfall at all, and what kind of climate does that make it?

Answer

Lima, Peru — a coastal desert climate: the cold offshore current stabilises the air, so the coast stays extremely dry despite facing the Pacific.

Q13. What does a cadastral map record that a topographic map does not?

Answer

Land ownership: the boundaries, dimensions and identifiers of individual land parcels, each linked to its ownership and legal records.

Q14. What is a "cadastre," and why do surveyors warn that ambiguous old descriptions cause trouble?

Answer

A cadastre is a public record of the value, extent and ownership of land, kept as a basis for taxation. Because cadastral boundaries follow the legal descriptions in deeds, vague old descriptions become the boundary disputes of the future.
