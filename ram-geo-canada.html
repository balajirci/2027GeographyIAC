Canada — The Arctic

From Ram's Canada readings (Set 1, 2026-27 · IAC Canada pack)

Study notes

  • Hans Island — location & size: an uninhabited island of 1.2 square kilometres (Tartupaluk in Inuktitut and Greenlandic) in the Nares Strait between Ellesmere Island and northern Greenland, roughly equidistant from both shores and within 12 miles of each.
  • Hans Island — the dispute: it began in 1973, when Canada and Denmark tried to draw a boundary through the Nares Strait and found both countries could credibly claim the island; a 1933 League of Nations court decision awarding it to Denmark carried little weight once the League fell apart.
  • Hans Island — the "Whiskey War": beginning in the 1980s, the two countries took turns staking their claim by planting flags and leaving bottles of liquor — whiskey from Canada, schnapps from Denmark; in 1984 the Danish minister left a flag, a bottle of brandy and a note reading "Welcome to the Danish island," while Canadians leave Canadian Club and a "Welcome to Canada" sign.
  • Hans Island — the settlement: the treaty signed on June 14, 2022, in Ottawa divides the island roughly in half, creating a land border between Canada and Denmark and the world's longest maritime boundary at nearly 4,000 kilometres; Inuit of both countries keep free movement about the island and its surrounding ice and waters, an important hunting ground since the 14th century.
  • Parry Channel — the route: a sea passage running east to west through the Arctic islands, named for explorer W.E. Parry: it begins at Lancaster Sound, passes through Barrow Strait, leads into Viscount Melville Sound and reaches the Beaufort Sea through M'Clure Strait.
  • Parry Channel — blockage & divide: permanent pack ice in M'Clure Strait is impassable, forcing ships making the Northwest Passage to detour far to the south; the channel marks the geological divide between the Precambrian rocks of the Canadian Shield to the south and the sedimentary rocks forming the Arctic islands to the north.
  • Parry Islands — the group: an archipelago in Nunavut and the Northwest Territories, part of the Queen Elizabeth Islands, lying south and west of Ellesmere Island; the major islands are Devon, Cornwallis, Bathurst, Melville and Prince Patrick.
  • Devon Island: the largest of the Parry Islands (55,247 sq km, about 515 km long), an ice-covered plateau rising to a maximum elevation of 1,920 metres in the east; discovered by William Baffin in 1616 and formerly named North Devon.
  • Melville Island: one of the largest Parry Islands (42,149 sq km, about 320 km long), divided between the Northwest Territories and Nunavut; it has no human habitation but supports musk-oxen and has natural-gas deposits; discovered by Sir William Parry in 1819 and named for Robert Saunders Dundas, 2nd Viscount Melville, then first lord of the Admiralty.
  • Cornwallis Island: a low-lying island of 10,000 sq km situated between Bathurst and Devon islands, with no large peaks or ice caps; east and south of Disappointment Bay to Helen Haven, bold bluffs drop as much as 250 m to the sea.
  • Resolute Bay (Qausuittuq): on the south central coast of Cornwallis Island, the second most-northerly community in Canada; established in 1947 as a weather station and airstrip, with four Inuit families relocated there from Québec in 1953 and five more two years later; today's population is 215.

Self-test

Q1. Where is Hans Island, and how large is it?

Answer

It sits in the Nares Strait between Ellesmere Island and northern Greenland, roughly equidistant from both; it is an uninhabited island of 1.2 square kilometres.

Q2. When did the Hans Island dispute begin, and why?

Answer

In 1973, when Canada and Denmark attempted to draw a boundary through the Nares Strait and found that both countries could credibly lay claim to the island.

Q3. Why did the Hans Island dispute become known as the "Whiskey War"?

Answer

Beginning in the 1980s, Canada and Denmark took turns staking their claim to the island by planting flags and leaving bottles of liquor — whiskey from Canada and schnapps from Denmark.

Q4. What did the treaty of June 14, 2022 do?

Answer

It divided Hans Island roughly in half, creating a land border between Canada and Denmark and the world's longest maritime boundary at nearly 4,000 kilometres, while ensuring Inuit of both countries can move freely about the island and surrounding ice and waters.

Q5. Trace the Parry Channel from east to west.

Answer

It begins at Lancaster Sound, passes through Barrow Strait, leads into Viscount Melville Sound, and finally reaches the Beaufort Sea through M'Clure Strait.

Q6. Why can ships not sail the full length of the Parry Channel on a Northwest Passage voyage?

Answer

The permanent pack ice in M'Clure Strait is an impassable obstacle, forcing ships to detour far to the south.

Q7. Which is the largest of the Parry Islands, and what are its key facts?

Answer

Devon Island — 55,247 sq km and about 515 km long, an ice-covered plateau rising to 1,920 metres in the east; it was discovered by William Baffin in 1616 and was formerly named North Devon.

Q8. What is Resolute Bay, and when was it established?

Answer

Resolute Bay (Qausuittuq), on the south central coast of Cornwallis Island, is the second most-northerly community in Canada; it was established in 1947 as a weather station and airstrip.

Canada — Atlantic & the East

From Ram's Canada readings (Set 1, 2026-27 · IAC Canada pack)

Study notes

  • Cape Breton — the link: Cape Breton was an island not connected to mainland Nova Scotia until the Canso Causeway linked it to the rest of North America in 1955; the 7,000-foot (2,100-metre) causeway carries rail and Trans-Canada Highway traffic.
  • Cape Breton — colonial past: it was a separate colony from mainland Nova Scotia from 1784 till 1820, and was part of Nova Scotia when Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Ontario and Quebec joined in 1867 to form Canada.
  • Strait of Canso: located in northeastern Nova Scotia, it separates Cape Breton Island from the mainland and links Chedabucto Bay (an inlet of the Atlantic Ocean) to St. Georges Bay and the Northumberland Strait.
  • Northumberland Strait: a tidal water body between Prince Edward Island and the coast of eastern New Brunswick and northern Nova Scotia.
  • The Mi'kmaq — early presence: Mi'kmaq oral tradition holds that they were living in Newfoundland prior to European contact, and there is historical evidence of them there by the 16th century, with increasing references in the 17th century.
  • The Mi'kmaq — "Domain of Islands": during the 16th and 17th centuries the Mi'kmaq created a "Domain of Islands" in the Gulf of St. Lawrence; adopting the small European sailing boat, the shallop (or chaloupe), Mi'kmaq traders built a network of exchange ranging from the Strait of Belle Isle to the coasts of Massachusetts, acting as middlemen in the exchange of European goods for furs.
  • Avalon Peninsula — deep past: the Avalon was part of the European continent 400 million years ago and has since been etched by glaciers and flooded by oceans, forming cliffs, coves and numerous bays.
  • Avalon Peninsula — settlement: Irish and English colonists came as early as the 1600s, and the Avalon was one of the first areas of the province to be settled; it is known for sea bird colonies, herds of caribou, whales and icebergs.
  • Cape Spear: on the Avalon Peninsula, the most easterly point of land in North America, with a restored 160-year-old lighthouse.

Self-test

Q1. When was Cape Breton Island first connected to the mainland, and by what structure?

Answer

In 1955, by the Canso Causeway — a 7,000-foot (2,100-metre) link carrying rail and Trans-Canada Highway traffic.

Q2. What does the Strait of Canso separate, and which waters does it link?

Answer

It separates Cape Breton Island from mainland Nova Scotia, and it links Chedabucto Bay, an inlet of the Atlantic Ocean, to St. Georges Bay and the Northumberland Strait.

Q3. Between which landmasses does the Northumberland Strait lie?

Answer

It is a tidal water body between Prince Edward Island and the coast of eastern New Brunswick and northern Nova Scotia.

Q4. How did Cape Breton's colonial status change between 1784 and 1867?

Answer

It was a separate colony from mainland Nova Scotia from 1784 till 1820, and was part of Nova Scotia when Nova Scotia joined New Brunswick, Ontario and Quebec in 1867 to form Canada.

Q5. What was the Mi'kmaq "Domain of Islands," and over what area did their trading network range?

Answer

A network of exchange the Mi'kmaq built across the Gulf of St. Lawrence in the 16th and 17th centuries, ranging from the Strait of Belle Isle between Newfoundland and Labrador to the coasts of Massachusetts, with Mi'kmaq traders acting as middlemen exchanging European goods for furs.

Q6. Which European vessel did Mi'kmaq traders adopt for their exchange network?

Answer

The shallop (or chaloupe), a small European sailing boat.

Q7. What distinction does Cape Spear hold?

Answer

Cape Spear, on the Avalon Peninsula, is the most easterly point of land in North America, and it has a restored 160-year-old lighthouse.

Canada — St. Lawrence, Great Lakes & Niagara

From Ram's Canada readings (Set 1, 2026-27 · IAC Canada pack)

Study notes

  • The system: the St. Lawrence River and Seaway is a hydrographic system of east-central North America running some 2,500 miles (4,000 kilometres) from the headwaters that flow into Lake Superior to Cabot Strait and the Atlantic Ocean — an international, multiprovincial system draining the heart of the continent.
  • Three sectors: upstream lies the Great Lakes region; the centre runs from the eastern outflow of Lake Ontario near Kingston to the Île d'Orléans just downstream from Quebec City; from there the system broadens into the St. Lawrence estuary and then the Gulf of St. Lawrence, passing Anticosti Island, to Cabot Strait between Newfoundland and Nova Scotia.
  • An ancient valley: the St. Lawrence occupies a geologically old depression involving the Canadian Shield, the Appalachian Mountains and the sedimentary rock platform; the Champlain Sea flooded the depression from about 13,000 to 9,500 years ago, and the riverlike St. Lawrence was established about 6,000 years ago.
  • The Seaway: a massive navigational project undertaken jointly by Canada and the United States and completed in 1959; the official seaway consists of the 186-mile stretch of the St. Lawrence River between Montreal and Lake Ontario plus the Welland Canal (connecting Lakes Ontario and Erie), while the entire Great Lakes–St. Lawrence system, some 2,340 miles from Duluth, Minnesota to the Atlantic, has 9,500 miles of navigable waterways.
  • The opening: the Seaway was officially opened on June 26, 1959, in a ceremony presided over by U.S. President Dwight D. Eisenhower and Queen Elizabeth II; the icebreaker D'Iberville had begun the first transit in April 1959.
  • Seaway traffic: the waterway moves bulk commodities — grain from the Canadian Prairies and the American Midwest travels east to lower St. Lawrence terminals, while ships often refill with iron ore from Quebec and Labrador for steel mills at Hamilton, Nanticoke and Sault Ste. Marie and American centres such as Cleveland, Detroit and Chicago; more than 2.5 billion tonnes of cargo moved on the system in its first 50 years, and lakes-only vessels account for 70 to 80 percent of its traffic.
  • Thousand Islands: St. Lawrence Islands National Park, established in 1904 near Kingston, Ontario, comprises 24 islands, 129 islets and 8 mainland tracts in the Frontenac Axis; the islands are the traditional territory of the Haudenosaunee and the Mississauga Anishinaabe.
  • Niagara River: about 58 kilometres (36 mi) long, it is the natural outlet from Lake Erie to Lake Ontario; the elevation between the two lakes is about 99 metres (326 ft), half occurring at the Falls themselves; the river drains approximately 684,000 sq km — and it actually flows north.
  • Horseshoe Falls: the Canadian Horseshoe Falls drops an average of 57 metres (188 ft) with a crest line approximately 670 metres (2,200 ft) wide; the plunge pool beneath is 35 metres deep, and the deepest section of the river, just below the Falls, is 52 metres — equal to the height of the Falls above.
  • American Falls & the channels: the American Falls ranges from 21 to 34 metres in height to the top of its talus slope, with a crest about 260 metres wide; at Grand Island the river divides into the west (Canadian or Chippawa) Channel, which carries approximately 60% of the total flow, and the east (American or Tonawanda) Channel.
  • Power & colour of the water: more than 168,000 cubic metres of water go over the crestline every minute during peak hours; the Falls have moved back seven miles in 12,500 years; the river's startling green colour comes from dissolved salts and "rock flour," with an estimated 60 tons of dissolved minerals swept over the Falls every minute.
  • Great Lakes water: the Great Lakes form the world's largest surface freshwater system, about 18 percent of the world's supply; today less than one percent of their water is renewable on an annual basis — the rest is a legacy of the last ice age, or "fossil" water.
  • Windsor: a city and the seat of Essex County in southern Ontario, situated on the left (south) bank of the Detroit River, opposite Detroit, Michigan — Windsor belongs on the Detroit River, not associated with Lake Erie or Lake St. Clair.

Self-test

Q1. Roughly how long is the St. Lawrence system, and what does it connect?

Answer

Some 2,500 miles (4,000 kilometres); it connects the headwaters flowing into Lake Superior with Cabot Strait, leading into the Atlantic Ocean, crossing the interior of the North American continent.

Q2. When was the St. Lawrence Seaway officially opened, and who presided over the ceremony?

Answer

On June 26, 1959 — the ceremony was presided over by U.S. President Dwight D. Eisenhower and Queen Elizabeth II.

Q3. Of what does the official St. Lawrence Seaway consist?

Answer

The 186-mile stretch of the St. Lawrence River between Montreal and Lake Ontario, and the Welland Canal, which connects Lakes Ontario and Erie.

Q4. Which cargoes dominate Seaway traffic, and in which directions do they move?

Answer

Bulk commodities: grain moves east from the Canadian Prairies and American Midwest to terminals on the lower St. Lawrence for export, and ships often backhaul iron ore from Quebec and Labrador west to steel mills such as Hamilton, Nanticoke, Sault Ste. Marie, Cleveland, Detroit and Chicago.

Q5. What is the Niagara River, and how long is it?

Answer

It is the natural outlet from Lake Erie to Lake Ontario, about 58 kilometres (36 mi) long.

Q6. Give the key measurements of the Canadian Horseshoe Falls.

Answer

It drops an average of 57 metres (188 ft), its crest line is approximately 670 metres (2,200 ft) wide, and more than 168,000 cubic metres of water go over the crestline every minute during peak hours.

Q7. About 500 waterfalls are taller than Niagara — so what makes Niagara Falls so remarkable?

Answer

The combination of height and volume of water; the tallest falls in the world, such as Venezuela's Angel Falls at 979 metres, carry very little water by comparison.

Q8. Where exactly is Windsor, and what mistake does the reading warn against?

Answer

Windsor is on the left (south) bank of the Detroit River, opposite Detroit, Michigan; it should be considered a city on the Detroit River and not associated with Lake Erie or Lake St. Clair.

Canada — Parks, Borders & Shared Places

From Ram's Canada readings (Set 1, 2026-27 · IAC Canada pack)

Study notes

  • International Peace Garden — founding: dedicated on July 14, 1932, with some 50,000 persons present, on the border between Manitoba and North Dakota.
  • The cairn: the garden's first boundary marker, built of stones gathered from North Dakota and Manitoba and inscribed with the pledge that the two nations dedicate the garden and will not take up arms against one another.
  • The flags: the cairn was first flanked by the Stars and Stripes and the Union Jack; in 1945 the Union Jack was replaced by the Red Ensign, and on February 15, 1965, the present Maple Leaf flag was adopted and has flown there since.
  • Building the garden: amid the Great Depression, the U.S. Civilian Conservation Corps was engaged in 1934 under the National Park Service — it fenced the United States acreage, cleared bush land, built lagoons and constructed the first building, the Lodge, made of native stone from North Dakota and logs from the Riding Mountain area of Manitoba.
  • Two lakes, two honourees: crescent-shaped Lake Udall was named in honour of W. V. Udall, publisher of the Boissevain Recorder and an ardent promoter of the Garden; the larger Lake Stormon, built on the Canadian side, honours the American supporter John A. Stormon, who gave over forty years of service to the Garden.
  • Waterton-Glacier International Peace Park: in 1932, Waterton Lakes National Park (Alberta, Canada) was combined with Glacier National Park (Montana, United States) to form the world's first International Peace Park.
  • Waterton-Glacier — character: spanning the Province of Alberta and the State of Montana, the park is exceptionally rich in plant and mammal species and in prairie, forest, alpine and glacial features.
  • Pelee Island — the superlative: Pelee Island, in Lake Erie, is the most southerly populated place in Canada, and because it rests at the same latitude as northern California, its climate is ideal for agriculture.
  • Pelee Island — island life: within its 42 square kilometres live 300 year-round residents, swelling to about 1,500 in summer and up to 3,000 on long weekends; the island is an important flyway for migrating birds and butterflies, and its farms grow soybeans, wheat and specialty corn, though it is best known for its vineyards.

Self-test

Q1. When was the International Peace Garden dedicated, and how many people attended?

Answer

On July 14, 1932, with some 50,000 persons present.

Q2. Of what is the Peace Garden cairn built, and what does its inscription pledge?

Answer

It is built of stones gathered from North Dakota and Manitoba; the inscription records the two nations dedicating the garden and pledging that, as long as men shall live, they will not take up arms against one another.

Q3. Which flags have flown on the Canadian side of the Peace Garden cairn, and when did they change?

Answer

First the Union Jack; it was replaced by the Red Ensign in 1945, and the present Maple Leaf flag, adopted on February 15, 1965, has flown from the flagstaff since that date.

Q4. Which two parks were combined in 1932 to form the world's first International Peace Park?

Answer

Waterton Lakes National Park in Alberta, Canada, and Glacier National Park in Montana, United States.

Q5. Who did much of the early construction work at the International Peace Garden?

Answer

The U.S. Civilian Conservation Corps, engaged in 1934 under the supervision of the National Park Service — it fenced the United States acreage, cleared bush land, built lagoons and constructed the first building, the Lodge.

Q6. What record does Pelee Island hold, and what is it best known for?

Answer

It is the most southerly populated place in Canada; the island in Lake Erie is an important flyway for migrating birds and butterflies and is best known for its vineyards.

Canada — Self-Test (all topics)

From Ram's Canada readings (Set 1, 2026-27 · IAC Canada pack)

Q1. By what name is Hans Island known in Inuktitut and Greenlandic?

Answer

Tartupaluk.

Q2. What did the Danish minister leave on Hans Island during his 1984 visit?

Answer

He planted a Danish flag and left a note saying "Welcome to the Danish island," along with a bottle of brandy.

Q3. For whom was Melville Island named, and who discovered it?

Answer

It was named for Robert Saunders Dundas, 2nd Viscount Melville, then first lord of the Admiralty; it was discovered by Sir William Parry in 1819.

Q4. What geological divide does the Parry Channel mark?

Answer

It marks the divide between the Precambrian rocks of the Canadian Shield to the south and the sedimentary rocks forming the Arctic islands to the north.

Q5. Between which two islands does Cornwallis Island lie?

Answer

Between Bathurst Island and Devon Island.

Q6. Which four colonies joined in 1867 to form Canada?

Answer

Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Ontario and Quebec.

Q7. The Avalon Peninsula was part of which continent 400 million years ago?

Answer

The European continent.

Q8. According to Mi'kmaq oral tradition, when were the Mi'kmaq living in Newfoundland?

Answer

Prior to European contact.

Q9. Which vessel began the first transit of the St. Lawrence Seaway, and when?

Answer

The icebreaker D'Iberville, in April 1959.

Q10. How much of the Great Lakes' water is renewable each year, and what is the rest called?

Answer

Less than one percent is renewable on an annual basis (from precipitation and groundwater); the rest is a legacy from the last ice age, called "fossil" water.

Q11. What gives the Niagara River its startling green colour?

Answer

Dissolved salts and "rock flour" — very finely ground rock picked up mainly from the limestone bed — with an estimated 60 tons of dissolved minerals swept over the Falls every minute.

Q12. How far has Niagara Falls moved in the past 12,500 years?

Answer

It has moved back seven miles, making it possibly the fastest-moving waterfall in the world.

Q13. At Grand Island, which channel of the Niagara River carries about 60 percent of the flow, and what is it called?

Answer

The west channel, known as the Canadian or Chippawa Channel; the east channel is the American or Tonawanda Channel.

Q14. Whom do Lake Udall and Lake Stormon at the International Peace Garden honour?

Answer

Lake Udall honours W. V. Udall, publisher of the Boissevain Recorder and a promoter of the Garden; Lake Stormon, on the Canadian side, honours the American supporter John A. Stormon, who served the Garden for over forty years.

Q15. How large is Pelee Island, and how does its population change in summer?

Answer

The island covers 42 square kilometres; its 300 year-round residents swell to about 1,500 people in summer, and up to 3,000 on long weekends.

Q16. Windsor is the seat of which county?

Answer

Essex County, in southern Ontario.
