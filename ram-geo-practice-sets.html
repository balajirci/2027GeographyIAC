Section C — Short Answer (model answers)

1. The Danube, the Inn, and the Ilz meet at Passau. Its baroque architecture (rebuilt after the 1662 fire by Italian architects), narrow alleys, and riverside setting give it a Mediterranean, canal-city feel — hence "Venice of Bavaria."

2. The Wadden Sea is a shallow, tidal coastal sea/mudflat area along the North Sea. It is bordered by the West Frisian Islands (Netherlands), the East Frisian Islands (Germany), and the North Frisian Islands (Germany and Denmark). 3. Usedom is split between two countries: about 80% of the island belongs to Germany, while its eastern tip belongs to Poland — one of very few German islands with an international land border across it. 4. The Mittelland Canal is an artificial waterway crossing central/northern Germany that connects several rivers (including the Rhine, Weser, and Elbe basins), allowing barges to move goods across the country without a direct river link. 5. Denmark, Poland, Czech Republic, Austria, Switzerland, France, Luxembourg, Belgium, and the Netherlands. 6. The North German Plain is low, flat lowland (mostly under 600 ft) good for farming and easy travel; the South German Hills/Uplands are higher, hillier terrain with forests, river valleys, and eventually the Alps near the Austrian border. 7. The Berlin Wall was a heavily guarded concrete barrier built in 1961 by East Germany to stop citizens from fleeing to democratic West Berlin. Swift political reform and mass protests in East Germany led to the wall being opened in November 1989. 8. The Rhine flows through Germany's industrial heartland, connecting major cities (Cologne, Düsseldorf, Mainz) and ports to the North Sea, making it one of Europe's busiest routes for shipping raw materials and goods. 9. The Zugspitze is Germany's highest peak, at about 2,962 m (9,717 ft), located in the Bavarian Alps in the state of Bavaria, on the border with Austria. 10. The Bavarian Forest National Park (Germany, established 1970 — Germany's first national park) and the Šumava National Park (Czech Republic) sit side-by-side across the border. 11. The Kiel Canal cuts across the base of the Jutland Peninsula in Schleswig-Holstein, connecting the North Sea and the Baltic Sea so ships can avoid sailing around Denmark. 12. "Bundesländer" (or Länder) means "federal states" — the political divisions that together make up the Federal Republic of Germany. There are 16 of them today.

Section 3: Short Answer

1. "Iran" means "land of the Aryans" — Aryan peoples migrated to the region from Central Asia around 1000 B.C., and the name reflects that heritage. 2. It is the only sea passage from the Persian Gulf to the open ocean, and a large share of the world's oil shipments pass through it, making it a critical global chokepoint. 3. The Greater Tunb, Lesser Tunb, and Abu Musa islands are claimed by both Iran and the UAE. Iran has controlled them since 1971, after British forces withdrew, while the UAE (through the emirates of Ras al-Khaimah and Sharjah) says Iran forcibly seized them just before the UAE's founding. 4. The Caspian Sea is landlocked with no outlet to an ocean, so the fresh water that flows in gradually evaporates, leaving behind salt and making the water taste salty. 5. Western mountains: dry, hot summer and freezing-cold winter. Central plateau: dry with a hot summer and cooler winter, with water coming mainly from melting mountain snow. Northern Caspian coast: warm and wet. 6. The mountain ranges crossing Iran are growing as tectonic plates collide underground, causing frequent earthquakes; the December 26, 2003 earthquake destroyed the ancient Citadel of Bam and killed more than 26,000 people. 7. Cyrus the Great founded the Achaemenid Empire; Pasargadae was his original capital, and Persepolis was built as a later capital by Darius the Great. 8. The Parthian Empire was overthrown in A.D. 224 by the Persian noble Ardashir I, who founded the Sassanian dynasty.

9. Shiites believe that only direct descendants of Muhammad are legitimate Islamic rulers, while Sunnis believe successors could be chosen from among his closest companions, not necessarily his direct relatives. 10. The Kopet Dag is a mountain chain that forms part of the border between Iran and Turkmenistan; its name means "many mountains" in Turkmen. 11. Iran is a hot country with very few large rivers; other than the Shatt al-Arab, the Karun is the only river wide enough for cargo and passenger boats, so water is a limited and valuable resource. 12. The Shah fled the country, Ayatollah Khomeini returned from exile, and Iranians voted to found the Islamic Republic of Iran, with Khomeini becoming the wali faqih (supreme leader).

Section 4: Long / Analytical (sample answers — accept reasonable variations)

1. The Zagros Mountains stretch across western Iran from Azerbaijan province in the northwest to the Persian Gulf in the southeast, with peaks often topping 9,600 ft and the highest, Dena, reaching 14731 ft / 4490 m; heavy winter snow can make the range impassable. Zard Kuh, second highest is 13848 ft / 4221 m. Brittanica treats the highest point in the range as Mount Dena, elevation 14,465 feet (4,409 meters), located in the middle Zagros. Together the two ranges enclose Iran's central plateau, shape its climate zones, and are a major source of the country's river water from melting snow. 2. The Achaemenid Empire began around 553 B.C. when Cyrus the Great defeated the Medes and built an empire stretching from Egypt to Greece to Russia; Darius the Great later expanded it and built Persepolis. Alexander the Great conquered Persia in 334–330 B.C., and Greek rule lasted about 70 years before the Parni-led Parthians took over, ruling from about 247 B.C. to A.D. 224 and famously defeating a Roman army at Carrhae. In A.D. 224, the Persian noble Ardashir I overthrew the Parthians and founded the Sassanian dynasty, which ruled until Arab armies conquered the Sassanians in the 600s and introduced Islam to the region. 3. The Strait of Hormuz is the only outlet from the oil-rich Persian Gulf to the open ocean, so a large share of the world's oil shipments must pass through it, giving whoever controls its waters major leverage over global energy supplies. The nearby Tunb and Abu Musa islands sit right at the strait's entrance, so their disputed ownership (Iran vs. the UAE) is tied directly to control over shipping lanes and security in this narrow, strategically vital passage. 4. Iran has dramatic climate variety: the western Zagros Mountains are dry with hot summers and freezing winters; the central plateau is dry with hot summers, cooler winters, and water mainly from melting mountain snow; and the northern Caspian coast is warm and wet, supporting forests and agriculture. Because so much of the interior is desert (Dasht-e Kavir and Dasht-e Lut) or mountainous, most farming and dense population centers cluster near the Caspian coast, in river valleys, or on the plateau where irrigation and snowmelt provide water. 5. The 1979 Islamic Revolution occurred after growing opposition to Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi's Western-style modernization and his treatment of religious opponents like the Ayatollah Khomeini, who was arrested in 1963 and later exiled. In 1979 the Shah fled the country and Khomeini returned, and Iranians voted to establish the Islamic Republic, adopting Sharia-influenced governance under a wali faqih. The change amounted to a coup d'état in effect (a sudden, decisive shift in power), moving Iran from a monarchy allied with the West to a Shiite Islamic Republic, which led to strained relations with the U.S. and, soon after, war with Iraq. 6. Iran's national parks reflect its range of habitats: Golestan National Park in the northeast mixes forest, mountain passes, and dry plains and is home to wild sheep, gazelles, and Persian leopards; Kavir National Park sits on the edge of the Dasht-e Kavir desert and is the last home of the critically endangered Asiatic cheetah; and Lake Urmia National Park protects a large salt lake where no fish survive but flamingos and pelicans feed on brine shrimp. This mix of mountains, deserts, and wetlands lets very different species — from mountain-dwelling leopards to desert cheetahs to salt-lake birds — each find a niche suited to their needs.

Section 4: Long / Analytical (sample answers — accept reasonable variations)

1. Pico Bolívar (~16,427 ft / 5,007 m) is in the Cordillera de Mérida, part of the Andes in western Venezuela, and is Venezuela's highest point. Mount Roraima (~9,094 ft / 2,772 m) sits in the Pakaraima/Guiana Highlands at the tripoint of Venezuela, Guyana, and Brazil, and is much lower — it is Guyana's highest point, not Venezuela's. Roraima is famous for its dramatic flat-topped tepui shape and its role in inspiring fiction, but Pico Bolívar's Andean elevation is far greater, which is why it — not Roraima — is Venezuela's national high point. 2. Positives: Guri Dam supplies about 70% of Venezuela's electricity, saves roughly 300,000 barrels of oil a day, is considered a relatively "green"/low-carbon energy source, created Guri Lake for recreation, and allows electricity exports to Colombia and Brazil. Negatives: it flooded 4,300 sq. km of rainforest, displaced indigenous Pemón, Yekuana, and Karina communities, unbalanced local ecosystems, and Venezuela's heavy reliance on the single dam caused a nationwide blackout in 2008. Students may argue either way as long as they cite specific evidence from both sides. 3. In 1819 Simón Bolívar devised a plan to unite what are now Venezuela, Colombia, and Ecuador into one independent state, Gran Colombia. He achieved this in 1821 by defeating the Spanish at the Battle of Carabobo. However, regional differences proved too strong, and in 1830 Venezuela seceded from Gran Colombia and became a fully independent republic, with José Antonio Páez as its first president; Ecuador also broke away the same year, leaving Nueva Granada (Colombia) as the remainder of Gran Colombia. 4. British Guiana became independent Guyana in 1966; Spanish Guiana became present-day southeastern Venezuela; French Guiana remains an overseas department of France; Dutch Guiana became independent Suriname in 1975; and Portuguese Guiana became the Brazilian state of Amapá. Together these five colonial claims explain why the coastline east of Venezuela today is divided among Guyana, Suriname, French Guiana, and Brazil, alongside Venezuela's own Guayana region. 5. Pico Bolívar is in the Cordillera de Mérida (Andes) in western Venezuela and is Venezuela's highest peak. Pico Simón Bolívar is in the Sierra Nevada de Santa Marta in Colombia and is one of that country's two candidates (along with Pico Cristóbal Colón) for its highest peak. A helpful memory trick: "Bolívar without 'Simón' = Venezuela's peak; 'Simón Bolívar' spelled out = Colombia's peak," or simply remembering that Pico Bolívar sits in Mérida (a Venezuelan state), while Pico Simón Bolívar sits in Santa Marta (a Colombian range). 6. A drop of water starts as part of the stream on top of Auyán-tepui, plunges over Angel Falls into Devil's Canyon (Cañón del Diablo), and joins the Churún River. The Churún River flows into the Carrao River, which joins the Caroní River (the river that also feeds the Guri Dam). The Caroní River then flows into the Orinoco River, Venezuela's largest river, which carries the water east through the Orinoco Delta into the Atlantic Ocean.
