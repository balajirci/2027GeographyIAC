Field Work — Practice Set (September 2026)

AI-compiled from past CanGeo field papers · topo maps, scale, symbols

Part 1 — Multiple Choice

  1. A topographic map has a scale of 1:50,000. On the map, two towns are 4 cm apart. What is the actual distance between them? A) 2 km · B) 20 km · C) 200 km · D) 0.2 km
  2. On a topographic map, contour lines that are very close together indicate: A) A flat, level area · B) A steep slope · C) A body of water · D) A road or highway
  3. A field researcher records a compass bearing of 135° while walking toward a river. In which general direction are they walking? A) Northeast · B) Southeast · C) Southwest · D) Northwest
  4. A student is designing a field study to measure soil moisture across a farmer's field. Which sampling method would give the most reliable, unbiased results? A) Only testing soil near the edges of the field, since it is easiest to reach · B) Testing soil at evenly spaced points across a grid covering the whole field · C) Testing soil only in the spot that looks the driest · D) Testing soil once, in the middle of the field
  5. What is the primary purpose of a legend (or key) on a topographic map? A) To show the direction of magnetic north only · B) To explain what symbols, colors, and line patterns represent · C) To list the names of everyone who made the map · D) To show the exact population of towns on the map
  6. A GPS unit shows a location's coordinates as 45°N, 75°W. What do these numbers represent? A) Elevation and temperature · B) Latitude and longitude · C) Map scale and distance · D) Wind speed and direction
  7. A researcher wants to study how plant life changes from the edge of a forest to its center. Which field method is best suited to this? A) A single quadrat placed at random · B) A transect line with sampling points at intervals · C) An aerial photograph only · D) A one-time interview with a park ranger
  8. Which of the following is an example of primary data in a field work study? A) Rainfall totals copied from a government website · B) Temperature readings taken by the student with a thermometer in the field · C) A satellite image found online · D) A textbook description of a biome
  9. On a map, the contour interval is 10 metres. If a hiker crosses 5 contour lines while climbing a hill, how much elevation have they gained? A) 5 m · B) 10 m · C) 50 m · D) 100 m

Part 2 — Short Answer

  1. Explain why it is important to record the date, time, and weather conditions when collecting field data.
  2. A student wants to measure how the temperature changes as they walk from a paved parking lot into a nearby forest. Describe the method they should use, including what equipment they need and how often they should record data.
  3. What is the difference between a topographic map and a political map? Give one field work situation where a topographic map would be more useful.
  4. Describe one way a researcher could reduce bias when choosing sample sites for a field study.
  5. Why might a field researcher use both a compass and a GPS device instead of relying on just one?

Part 3 — Straight Questions

  1. What tool is used to measure the direction (bearing) you are facing in the field?
  2. What do contour lines on a topographic map connect?
  3. What is the term for a straight line along which a researcher takes samples at regular intervals?
  4. What do we call data that a researcher collects themselves, firsthand, in the field?
  5. On a map with a scale of 1:100,000, how many real-world kilometres does 1 cm represent?
  6. What is the name of the imaginary line at 0° latitude?
  7. What instrument would you use to measure wind speed during a field study?
Answers (tap to reveal)

Part 1 — Multiple Choice

  1. A) 2 km — 4 cm × 50,000 = 200,000 cm; 200,000 cm ÷ 100,000 = 2 km.
  2. B) A steep slope — Closely spaced contour lines mean elevation changes quickly over a short distance. Widely spaced lines indicate flatter terrain.
  3. B) Southeast — Bearings are measured clockwise from North; 135° falls between East (90°) and South (180°).
  4. B) Grid sampling — A systematic grid sample across the whole field avoids bias and gives a representative picture of soil moisture throughout the area.
  5. B) — A legend explains the meaning of the symbols, colors, and patterns used on the map, so the reader can correctly interpret features like water, forests, roads, and elevation.
  6. B) Latitude and longitude — The first number (with N or S) is latitude, position north or south of the equator; the second (with E or W) is longitude, position east or west of the prime meridian.
  7. B) A transect line — A transect is a line along which observations are recorded at set intervals, ideal for studying gradual change across a distance, like forest edge to center.
  8. B) — Primary data is collected firsthand by the researcher. The other options are secondary data — collected by someone else and reused.
  9. C) 50 m — 5 contour lines × 10 m contour interval = 50 m of elevation gained.

Part 2 — Short Answer (model answers)

  1. These details give context to the data and show conditions that might have influenced the results (e.g., temperature or precipitation affecting soil moisture readings). They also let the study be repeated, or compared with other data collected under similar or different conditions.
  2. Set up a transect line from the parking lot into the forest and take temperature readings at regular, evenly spaced intervals (e.g., every 10 metres) using a thermometer. Record the distance, temperature, and a brief description of the surface/land cover at each point, keeping the time of day and other conditions consistent.
  3. A topographic map shows physical features such as elevation, landforms, and natural features using contour lines; a political map shows boundaries such as countries, provinces, or cities. A topographic map is more useful for planning a hiking route, since it shows slope, elevation change, and terrain.
  4. Use a systematic or random sampling method (such as a grid or randomly generated coordinates) rather than choosing sites based on convenience or a hunch about the expected results. This ensures the sample better represents the whole study area.
  5. The two serve as backups for each other: a GPS can lose signal or run out of battery, while a compass does not rely on satellites or power. Using both increases reliability and accuracy, and a compass helps confirm that GPS readings make sense.

Part 3 — Straight Questions

  1. A compass.
  2. Points of equal elevation.
  3. A transect.
  4. Primary data.
  5. 1 km.
  6. The equator.
  7. An anemometer.

Field Work — Set 2: Indoor Fieldwork Test (Junior)

Test + sources + official answers

From the Balkan International Geography Olympiad, Iași, Romania, 2018 (Junior). The test is built around three observation points; you receive the paper and source papers for each point separately and hand them back before moving on. About 30 minutes per observation point. Fieldwork is worth 18 points (6 per observation point); the indoor activity is worth 22 points. Write answers in the box on the paper. Bilingual dictionaries are allowed; talking with other students and using devices are not.

Subject 1 — Observation Point 1: The Brownfield (Source A)

The area marked A on Source A is a brownfield — previously developed land, not currently in use, formerly industrial or commercial, with known or suspected pollution. Given: Iași has about 350,000 inhabitants (500,000 with the metropolitan area); about 70,000 people live within a 600 m buffer zone of the site; the districts to the South, South East and South West have poor access to facilities (no green areas, no supermarkets, no hospitals, no office centres); the site covers 30 ha; the city plans a sports centre, a regional hospital, a village livable museum, and a multimodal transportation centre. The site lies south-east of the UAIC tower, near the main train station.

  1. [Map] 1a (1 point). Explain the process of industrial relocation in the past 30 years, using the keywords: accessibility, sustainability, pollution, land value.
  2. [Map] 1b (3 points). Propose a plan to rehabilitate the area using all the information provided. Offer arguments using the risks associated with the site today, the plan you propose, and the overall importance to the city.

Subject 2 — Observation Point 2: Neolithic Population (Source B)

  1. 2a (1 point). How did the demographics of the population change from the Paleolithic to the Neolithic?
  2. [Map] 2b (1 point). Using the map in Source B, indicate three regions with the maximum density of population, and present two common characteristics of their environment.
  3. 2c (3 points). Explain how the Cucuteni civilization influenced the close environment. (What was their ecological footprint? How did they affect the natural environment?)

Subject 3 — Observation Point 3: The Botanical Garden (Source 1)

The Botanical Garden (observation point 3) previously stood at three other locations — B, C, D — marked on Source 1.

  1. [Map] 3a (2 points). Identify 2 possible causes for the changing of the location of the Botanical Garden from point C to point D.
  2. [Map] 3b (2 points). Argue for the location you consider to be the best among the four alternatives.
  3. [Map] 3c (4 points). In terms of infrastructural development and social-communal activities, elaborate 2 strategic directions for the betterment of the lake in the Botanical Garden — one direction of each type.

Subject 4 — The University

All three observation points are managed by the Alexandru Ioan Cuza University of Iași, founded in 1860 — the first university of its kind in Romania, and the third largest, with more than 30,000 students.

  1. 4a (1 point). Write a one-sentence "vision statement" for a more attractive and dynamic University, taking into account the historical and cultural heritage of the institution and of the city.
  2. 4b (3 points). Elaborate 3 strategic directions which aim to attract more students to the University and are directly linked to the vision statement you proposed. Campus facilities and the social life of students should not be ignored.
Answers (tap to reveal)
  1. 1a. Industry has shifted from heavy industry to light, "footloose" industry that can locate almost anywhere, so the factors that decide location have changed. Old heavy industry was tied to physical factors — bulky raw materials, water or coal power, flat land. Today the decisive factors are socio-economic: accessibility (transport routes for bringing in materials and shipping products out — motorways, railways, airports, ports, and now internet communications), capital, government policy and incentives, and labour supply, which matters less than it did now that workers drive and production is mechanised. Heavy, polluting industry has been pushed out of inner-city sites by pollution and sustainability concerns and by government closures, while high inner-city land value encourages factories to sell up and relocate to cheaper out-of-town land with room to expand.
  2. 1b. No model answer is printed in the official key — the plan is marked on how completely it uses the information given. A full-marks plan should: deal with the site's risks first (suspected soil contamination from its industrial past must be investigated and cleaned up before public use); build the facilities the city already plans and the surrounding districts lack — the regional hospital, sports centre, museum, and multimodal transport centre beside the main station; serve the roughly 70,000 people within 600 m and the underserved southern districts (South, South East, South West) that have no green areas, supermarkets, hospitals, or office centres; and use the site's size (30 ha) and station-side accessibility as its importance to the whole city, not just the neighbourhood.
  3. 2a. The Neolithic Demographic Transition: with farming, population grew rapidly after millennia of near-stability (about 6 million people worldwide on the eve of agriculture). Sedentary farmers ate higher-calorie food and spent less energy carrying infants, so fertility rose first — the total fertility rate increased by about two births per woman, and birth intervals shortened. Mortality rose after it (crowded villages, poor sanitation and contaminated water, new diseases including zoonoses from domesticated animals), settling into the pre-industrial pattern of high birth and death rates with slow growth of roughly 0.1–0.2% per year. Carrying capacity jumped from about 0.05 people per km² under foraging to far higher densities, cemeteries show a surge in the proportion of 5–19-year-olds, and permanent villages of 150+ people appeared.
  4. 2b. Any three of: the Middle East (Levant), Central China, South-East Europe, the Nile Valley, the Andean region of South America. Two common environmental characteristics: fertile soils, and reliable water sources (rivers, springs, mineral springs); favourable sites for defence are also credited.
  5. 2c. Sedentary farming communities radically transformed their surroundings. They cleared the land around their settlements (axes for felling, later the plough), controlled which plants grew and which animals grazed, and built ever larger and more numerous permanent settlements — villages and cultivated fields became the dominant landscape. They reshaped water with reservoirs, canals, dikes, and sluices for storage and irrigation; built with sun-dried brick, wattle, and stone; stored food in pottery; and increasingly fortified settlements to protect stored food. The steadily growing human presence — more people, more cleared and managed land — is the ecological footprint.
  6. 3a. Any two of: the need for more space, to give a growing number of students a place to study and relax; inappropriate funding strategies that failed several times, forcing relocation; repurposing of the old area into a park and buildings serving administrative purposes for the University, as well as a canteen; the expansion of the limits of the city.
  7. 3b. The key argues for Location D: it has a larger space to accommodate numerous plant sections, with room to expand at its edge if required; it sits at the periphery of the city, so it is less polluted; it is already in a neighbourhood with numerous green spaces, blending into the scenery; and it lies in an area affected by landslides, where building housing or other buildings would not be risk-free — so a garden is a safe use of the land. A well-argued case for C (very close to the university, forming a compact "nucleus of scientific interest"; easily accessible; keeps the continuity of green, recreational areas in the Copou neighbourhood) or for A/B (easily accessible, city-centre locations favouring more visitors and higher entrance-fee income) also earns the points.
  8. 3c. Infrastructural development: recondition the road infrastructure and make it more pedestrian-friendly; recondition the long stairs from the entrance to the lake, introducing several levels where people can rest (thematic recreational places); introduce more "urban furniture" that blends seamlessly with the environment while serving a function (trash cans, directional signs, thematic benches). Social-communal activities: generate more partnerships with local administrative institutions; run awareness campaigns for local and foreign visitors.
  9. 4a. Any one sentence that combines the University's heritage with attractiveness and dynamism earns the point. Examples from the key: "University of Iași – inspired by illuminated students." A longer model credited in the key builds the sentence around high quality in research, education, and cooperation, responsibility for the development of society, global engagement, and an inspiring work environment.
  10. 4b. No model answer is printed in the official key. The three directions must each link back to the vision statement written in 4a, and together they must cover campus facilities and the social life of students, not just academics.

Field Work — Set 1: How the Test Works

Instructions & evaluation, condensed

How the day is structured

  • Morning = guided observation walk. You follow your group guide, explanations, and timetable along a planned route. The route is designed so that you see everything you will need in the afternoon — do not wander away from the group.
  • Afternoon = written fieldwork test, built entirely from the morning: your notes, sketches, and annotated maps are your study material, and you must bring them with you. The observation focus in this set: a former factory (the Ruše Nitrogen Factory), the town of Ruše, the uninhabited space between them, the slopes of Pohorje and Kobansko, the Drava River and its valley.
  • Annotate as you go. You are expected to write notes, sketch, and draw directly on the supplied maps and sources during the walk — that collected data is what the test questions draw on.

How the test paper is structured

  • 11 sections, maximum 40 points. Sections 1–10 are worth 3 points each — three tasks per section, 1 point per task. Section 11 is a single essay task worth 10 points.
  • Time allowed: 180 minutes. A calculator and bilingual dictionaries are permitted.
  • Answer in the spaces provided, and check the back of the leaf — a task may be printed on two sides.
  • Give only the required number of answers. If a question asks for 2 examples and you write more than 2, only the first 2 will be considered.

The three types of task

  • Type 1 — general impression: questions based on the overall impression you formed during the morning observations.
  • Type 2 — pointed-out facts and processes: questions on facts and processes that were specifically flagged in the instructions and by the guide during the walk.
  • Type 3 — new afternoon material: questions based on additional material handed out in the afternoon, connected to the morning's observations (maps, figures, source texts, timetables).

What the tasks ask you to do

  • Read landscapes from maps and figures: use a geological cross-section and map to decide which river terrace the oldest part of town arose on; explain why one area has one terrace more than another (the inside of a meander is an accumulation bank, so it can gain a terrace); identify rock types from the map (e.g., area C is conglomerate, a softer material).
  • Explain industrial location: name and explain four factors behind a factory's location (in the key: the energy of the Fala power station, the railway on an important route, a political decision, available labour including prisoners, flat land), then compare two candidate sites and explain a pollution pattern — the NW wind would spread the factory's polluted air to the east.
  • Calculate: at least one section is pure measurement from a map — measure the distance (approx. 400 m), take the height difference (30 m), and compute the gradient: 30 ÷ 400 = 0.075 = 7.5%, then judge whether the proposal is within limits.
  • Complete the map: some sections give you a town plan with a building block missing, or one building too many — you reconstruct it from the numbered grid and the map legend.
  • Classify the town: separate town-forming components (factory, railway, political decision, growing population, municipal centre, schools, shops…) from town-serving activities, with descriptions; then discuss the role of the railway at local, regional, and highway scale, working from the timetable (e.g., counting daily connections, journey times of 15–34 minutes, average speeds of 20–52 km/h).
  • Assess sectors and change: tourism (what the mountains offer — altitude, aspect/exposition, days of snow cover, temperatures for artificial snow — and its jobs, development, and out-migration effects), agriculture and forestry (products, transport, how intensively land is used), and suburbanization, where answers must be logical and supported by reasonable arguments from the sources.
  • The 10-point essay: development opportunities for the town. It is marked on the activity you propose, your arguments for the space it would occupy, your arguments for the needs of the region, and the general impression of the answer — the marking grid gives 1 point per element across the proposals, so every proposal needs its own space argument and needs argument.

What to observe during the walk (from the instructions)

  • Keep your maps live: mark your location on each map as you move, and draw the trace of your walking route on the large-scale town plans. Note where key features sit (e.g., the hydropower plant on the detailed map).
  • Estimate quantities: how big the factory area is, what percentage of its buildings and open areas are used today, the height of structures such as the plumbing tower, how far and how deep the river is from it, the altitude difference between the factory and the town centre. Dam facts given on site (foundation at 280 m altitude, barrier 16 m high) feed a judgement question about which area is at risk if it collapses.
  • Map the town centre: mark shops, inns, and restaurants; the post office, bridges over the stream, church, municipal house, and parks; hatch the areas of residential houses, apartment blocks, and old houses; flag abandoned and neglected buildings; note whether new buildings are going up and old ones are being renovated.
  • Watch traffic and people: traffic density and whether personal or freight traffic predominates; the class of cars; whether pedestrians and cyclists are safe; whether pedestrians are adults, children, or elderly, locals or tourists.
  • Read the land between stops: unpaved surfaces left and right of the road (fields, meadows, orchards, parks, recreational or abandoned areas); vegetation on the slopes and the level of the forest boundary; whether the town grew in a planned or random pattern; whether its layout is geometric or irregular.
  • Ask the judgement questions early: does the region have water-supply problems, and why? Are there enough shops and services — which activity is missing, which is redundant? Is there enough green space between the houses? What would interest a tourist here? How would the landscape look in winter? These impressions are exactly what Type 1 tasks test.
  • Use every document: check the station timetable against the copy in your materials, note disabled/pram accessibility and parking at the stop — small verifiable details are easy marks in the afternoon.
